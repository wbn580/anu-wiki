Studying at the Australian National University (ANU) is not always smooth sailing. Every year, large numbers of students — whether because they underestimated a course's difficulty or were hit by an unexpected event — face failing grades, supplementary exams or even academic warnings. Drawing on ANU's official policies and the accumulated experience of the student community, this article **systematically reviews fail-rate data across ANU colleges (such as CECS, CASS and CBE), the eligibility and process for supplementary exams, the special consideration policy, and how strict each college is, providing an academic risk early-warning and response guide for current students**, helping you spot risk early and make the right calls at critical moments.

## ANU College Fail Rates at a Glance: Which Is Toughest — CECS, CASS or CBE?

The nature of courses and assessment methods varies enormously between colleges, and fail rates show a clear gradient as a result. Based on feedback from past students and the difficulty profiles of each college's courses, the general picture is as follows.

**The College of Engineering and Computer Science (CECS)** has long been seen as the fail-rate "disaster zone". In particular, first- and second-year core courses in computer science, software engineering and electronic engineering see fail rates of 25% to 35% in some semesters. These courses are mostly closed-book written exams with heavy programming workloads and strict marking standards — miss a submission or underperform in the final, and you can easily slide into the danger zone of supplementary exams or even straight re-enrolment.

**The College of Business and Economics (CBE)** sits overall at a mid-to-high fail rate. In quantitatively assessed disciplines like accounting, finance and actuarial studies, individual courses see fail rates between 15% and 25%, while management and marketing courses, which rely heavily on group projects and essays, fail at a relatively lower 5% to 10%. Notably, CBE's large first-year foundation courses such as "Business Reporting and Decision Making" are deliberately graded with a wider spread because of the huge cohort, which makes weaker students more likely to brush against the fail line.

**The College of Arts and Social Sciences (CASS)** generally has low fail rates — most courses sit below 10%, and some essay-based courses approach zero. But that is no excuse to let your guard down: if you are found to have plagiarised, not only does the course count as zero, it can trigger a college-level academic misconduct investigation whose consequences are far more serious than an ordinary fail.

The ANU College of Law and the ANU College of Health & Medicine usually keep fail rates within 10%, because entry thresholds are high and the student body is highly self-disciplined. That said, law core courses often decide more than 70% of the final grade with a single "end-of-semester exam", concentrating the risk — a single slip-up is not an option.

## Supplementary Exam: Eligibility and Process in Detail

At ANU, a supplementary exam is not an automatic "statutory right" for students who fail — it is a remedy that colleges grant at their discretion under specific conditions. Generally speaking, the pass mark for undergraduate courses is 50, and when the final overall mark lands between 45 and 49, some colleges allow students to sit one supplementary exam. Master's programs apply tighter rules, and many courses simply require re-enrolment below 45 with no supplementary window.

![anu-wiki image](https://img.anu.wiki/留学-单校wiki/anu-colleges-fail-rates-supplementary-exam-special-consideration-guide-2026-1880x1253.jpg)

Applying for a supplementary exam normally requires meeting the following conditions:
- The course has a supplementary exam arrangement for the current semester (not all courses offer one);
- The final overall mark falls in the 45–49 range (a few colleges occasionally relax this to 44);
- All compulsory assignments were submitted on time, with no penalties for academic misconduct such as plagiarism;
- The student has no record of exam misconduct during the examination period.

The process in detail: after results are released, eligible students receive an invitation by email via Wattle or their ANU student mailbox, and must confirm participation within a very short window (2–3 business days). If you do not proactively check your email and confirm, the offer is treated as declined. The supplementary exam is generally held one to two weeks after the official results are released, and may take the form of an in-person written exam, an online test, or submission of remedial work. The final grade after passing a supplementary exam is normally capped at 50 (the pass line) and appears on the transcript as "passed by supplementary exam". If you fail the supplementary exam again, you must re-enrol in the course.

One thing to note: CECS is the strictest when reviewing supplementary exam eligibility. It not only restricts the mark range but also checks whether you have a prior fail record — repeated failures may lead to the supplementary exam being refused outright, with re-enrolment required instead. CBE is comparatively more flexible, but places are limited and are assessed on a first-come, first-served basis.

## Special Consideration: Which Situations Qualify?

When students are hit by an uncontrollable, unexpected event during exams or before a major assignment deadline, they can apply for a deferred exam or an extension through ANU's Special Consideration channel. The policy has a clearly defined scope, but the assessment process is rigorous.

Acceptable grounds mainly include:
- A sudden serious illness or injury (with a doctor's certificate that explicitly states the condition directly affects your ability to sit the exam or complete the assignment);
- Serious illness, death or major upheaval involving an immediate family member;
- Force majeure events such as being involved in a traffic accident, becoming a victim of crime, or experiencing a natural disaster;
- Other compelling circumstances, such as being required to appear in court to fulfil a legal obligation.

Commonly unacceptable reasons include: exam timetable clashes (due to personal travel plans), a mild cold or stress, inadequate preparation for the exam content, misreading the exam time, or traffic congestion that could have been avoided by leaving earlier.

Applications are submitted through ANU's eForm system and must be lodged within three working days of the event; submitting before the exam or assignment deadline is strongly recommended. The standard materials generally include: a completed application form, third-party supporting documents (medical certificates, police reports, etc.), and a personal statement explaining how the event affected your academic performance. All documents must be in English or accompanied by an official translation. Colleges notify applicants of the outcome by email within 5 to 10 working days.

Approval standards differ noticeably between colleges. CECS tends to follow the letter of the documentation: if a medical certificate does not describe in detail how the symptoms prevented you from sitting the exam, they will often ask for supplementary materials or refuse outright. CASS, whose assessments are mostly essays, commonly grants extensions instead of formal deferrals, taking a softer approach. CBE, for courses with dense exam schedules and large cohorts, usually sets a unified supplementary exam date and schedules all approved students into that sitting, making the process highly efficient.

## How Strict Is Each College? Comparing Supplementary Exam and Special Consideration Approval

Expressed as a rough "red line" ranking, the order is: **CECS ≥ College of Law > CBE > College of Health & Medicine > CASS**.

CECS brings "engineering precision" to every decision involving a grade: it barely budges on the supplementary exam mark range, and demands the most detailed third-party evidence for special consideration — students often call it one of the hardest colleges to deal with. The College of Law has few absolute failures, but its supplementary exam policy is highly opaque and sometimes disallows supplementary exams altogether, steering students toward re-enrolment or appeal, which safeguards the rigour of legal education.

CBE's handling leans toward process: as long as you meet the stated requirements and supply complete materials, approval odds are decent — but precisely because of that, supplementary exam places are tight. CASS converts most special consideration into essay extensions; as long as you talk to your lecturer in advance and provide evidence, they are usually accommodating, though extensions last at most 15 working days and you must make sure follow-up assignments are not snowball-affected.

There is also a common cross-cutting issue: students in cross-college double degrees must satisfy both colleges' supplementary exam or special consideration policies simultaneously. That means if you are doing a double degree across CECS and CBE, a failure means weighing the intersection of both sets of rules — and your actual position is more stringent.

## Academic Risk Early Warning: Which Courses Fail Easily, and How to Get Ahead of It

High-risk courses tend to share these features: compulsory first-year courses, subjects where a closed-book final exam decides more than 60% of the overall mark, and advanced courses with huge swings in assignment difficulty. Typical examples include CECS's "Introduction to Computer Systems" and "Algorithms and Data Structures", CBE's "Intermediate Financial Accounting" and "Corporate Finance", plus a number of advanced mathematics courses. These courses not only fail students at a high rate — passing the supplementary exam is often no easier than re-enrolling and retaking the course.

Prevention strategy boils down to three levels:
- **At course selection**: read the course outline and past reviews to understand the assessment format and weightings. If a course is 100% closed-book final exam with widespread fail rumours, and your own tolerance for pressure is average, stagger it across your semester plan rather than stacking two or more such "killer courses" at the same time.
- **Mid-semester**: keep an eye on your continuous assessment. The moment you see a midterm or first major assignment score below 50%, switch into emergency mode. Use the free tutoring at the ANU Academic Skills Centre, PASS (Peer-Assisted Study Sessions) programs, and lecturer consultation hours to close the knowledge gaps precisely.
- **Crisis management before exams**: if you genuinely have no confidence of passing and suspect your overall mark will hover around 45, prepare your supplementary exam materials in advance, learn your college's supplementary exam rules, and check Wattle and your email the moment results are released — do not miss the confirmation window.

## What to Do After a Fail: Supplementary Exam, Re-enrolment or Appeal?

The first step after failing is to calmly assess your mark band and policy eligibility. If your total is between 45 and 49, prioritise the supplementary exam — the path is set out above. If your mark is below 45, or the course explicitly offers no supplementary exam, re-enrolment is mandatory. Note: if you re-enrol in the same course twice and still fail, some colleges initiate an Academic Progress Review; in serious cases you may receive a Show Cause notice, or even face enrolment restrictions or exclusion risk.

![anu-wiki image](https://img.anu.wiki/留学-单校wiki/anu-colleges-fail-rates-supplementary-exam-special-consideration-guide-2026-1880x1146.jpg)

If you believe the grade involved a procedural error, bias or marking mistakes, you can go through the formal Appeal channel. The appeal window is usually 20 working days from the release of results, and requires detailed evidence showing the marking was unfair. This demands very strong supporting evidence and success is often not guaranteed, but at minimum it ensures the marking process is re-examined.

A common mistake is that many students, after failing, passively wait and miss the supplementary exam confirmation window, or never learn that special consideration can remedy past assessment items. Every student starting at ANU should read their own college's policy pages carefully at the start of the semester and enter the key dates into their personal calendar — manage risk in advance rather than letting risk manage you.

## FAQ

**How many times can I sit a supplementary exam for one course?**  
Each course normally offers only one supplementary exam opportunity. If you still fail the supplementary exam, the only way forward is to re-register for the course and pay full tuition to retake it. In very rare cases, where a college's policy allows up to two supplementary exams for a course, this is explicitly stated in the course outline — but such cases are extremely uncommon.

**How does a passed supplementary exam appear on my transcript?**  
In most cases, the passing grade shows as "50" with a "PX" (Supplementary Pass) notation. The notation stays on your transcript permanently, but whether it matters for further study or employment varies by institution and employer; generally, as long as your overall GPA is not seriously dragged down, the impact is limited.

**What if my special consideration application is rejected?**  
If your special consideration application is refused, you can request a review from your college's Student Administration Office within 5 working days, providing supplementary materials. If the review is also rejected, you must sit the original exam or submit the assignment by the original deadline. If that leads to a fail, you can then go through the supplementary exam or re-enrolment process. Keeping every email exchanged with the college is very important as evidence for any later appeal.

**If I suddenly feel unwell on the day of the final exam, should I sit the exam or apply for special consideration?**  
Sitting the exam through illness is strongly discouraged. If you take the exam unwell and complete it, you cannot claim special consideration afterwards even with a medical certificate — your grade will be calculated on your actual performance. The right approach is to seek medical attention immediately, obtain a detailed diagnosis certificate, submit a special consideration application as soon as possible, forgo the original exam, and sit the deferred or alternative assessment arranged afterwards.

## Summary

ANU colleges differ systematically in fail rates, supplementary exam availability and special consideration approval — CECS is the most iron-fisted, CBE has clear processes but fierce competition, and the arts and social sciences look easy-going yet hold zero tolerance for academic integrity breaches. Knowing all this is not about stoking anxiety; it is about giving you more agency in your academic life at ANU. Planning your course load early, monitoring grade signals in real time, and knowing the windows for supplementary exams and special consideration are the keys to getting through this top university smoothly. Wherever you are in your degree right now, spending an hour reading your college's policy pages is perhaps the most worthwhile investment you can make this academic year.
