## Finding a Rental Property

Start your search on widely used platforms that list residential leases across Australia. Large portals aggregate listings from real estate agents and private landlords, giving you a broad view of available homes, units, and apartments. Refine results by suburb, price range, number of bedrooms, and property type. Many students also find options through university accommodation services and local community noticeboards.

After identifying a suitable listing, register for the advertised open‑home time or request a private viewing. Competition in major cities can be strong, so aim to attend the first scheduled inspection if possible. Bring any documents the agent may want to see on the day, such as identification and proof of income.

## Physical Inspection Essentials

During the open house or private walk‑through, check every space thoroughly. Operate taps and the shower to confirm water pressure and hot water. Test light switches, power outlets, stovetop burners, and the oven. Open and close all doors and windows to verify they work smoothly and lock securely. Look for signs of dampness, mould, or pests, especially in corners, under sinks, and around window frames. If the property has a split‑system or ducted air‑conditioner, turn it on briefly to gauge heating and cooling performance. A slow walk‑around also lets you assess traffic noise, neighbouring sounds, and mobile phone reception inside the premises.

It is sensible to bring a notepad or use your phone to record any existing damage you spot. Photographs taken with the agent’s permission can support the condition report you will fill out at the start of the tenancy.

## Understanding the Lease Contract

A written residential tenancy agreement sets out the rights and duties of both the tenant and the landlord. Read every clause before signing, paying particular attention to:
- The fixed term length (commonly 6 or 12 months) and the date rent is payable.
- Whether you are allowed to sub‑let or transfer the lease.
- Who is responsible for utility connections and charges.
- Any special rules about common areas, pets, or modifications (like hanging pictures).

Agents must give you a copy of the lease and a condition report at the start of the tenancy. The condition report is the baseline record of the property’s state; complete it carefully and return it within the timeframe stated in your state’s legislation. If you discover a hidden issue later, document it in writing to the landlord or agent.

## Bond (Security Deposit) Rules

When you pay a rental bond it acts as security against damage or unpaid rent, but the money is not held by the landlord or agent. Each state and territory government operates an independent bond‑lodgement service — often called a Residential Tenancies Bond Authority — that holds the funds in trust for the duration of the tenancy. The bond amount is usually capped at the equivalent of four weeks’ rent for an unfurnished property, though the exact limit varies slightly by jurisdiction.

At the end of the lease, the bond is released only when both parties agree or after an order from a tribunal. Any claim against the bond must be supported by evidence, which is why your initial condition report and ongoing written correspondence matter. If a dispute arises, the matter can be taken to the relevant state or territory civil and administrative tribunal, which will decide how the bond is divided.

## FAQ

**What platforms can I use to find a rental in Australia?**
The two largest online property portals aggregate listings from agents and private landlords. University accommodation offices and local classifieds sites also carry rental advertisements.

**What should I check during a property inspection?**
Test plumbing, electrical fixtures, appliances, heating or cooling units, and all locks. Look for mould, pests, and structural damage. Record any defects you see so they are noted before you move in.

**How much bond can a landlord ask for?**
In most states the maximum bond is four weeks’ rent for an unfurnished home, though the precise cap depends on local tenancy law.

**Where is my bond held?**
Your bond is deposited with the official bond‑lodgement authority in the state or territory where you rent. The landlord or agent never keeps the money personally.

**How is the bond returned at the end of the lease?**
Both tenant and landlord must agree on the final amount. If they cannot agree, the state tribunal can make a binding decision after reviewing evidence such as the condition report and photos.
