## Introduction to Logic Chain Analysis

GMAT Critical Reasoning questions often test your ability to evaluate arguments. A useful technique is **logic chain analysis**, which involves breaking an argument into its core components: premises, evidence, and conclusion. By mapping these elements and their logical connections, you can more easily identify how a piece of information might weaken or strengthen an argument, or what assumption the argument depends on.

This guide focuses on three common question types: **weaken**, **strengthen**, and **assumption**. We'll demonstrate how to apply logic chain analysis using typical GMAT-style examples.

## Weaken Questions: Attacking the Link

Weaken questions ask you to choose the answer that most undermines the argument. In logic chain analysis, you look for the link between evidence and conclusion. A weaken answer often introduces information that breaks the assumed connection.

**Example Argument:**

- **Premise 1:** A local store increased its advertising budget by 20% last quarter.
- **Premise 2:** Sales also increased by 20% last quarter.
- **Conclusion:** Therefore, the advertising caused the sales increase.

**Logic Chain:**

1. [Premise] Advertising increase
2. [Premise] Sales increase
3. [Conclusion] Advertising caused sales increase

The unstated link is that no other factor contributed to the sales increase. A valid weaken answer might be: "During the same quarter, a major competitor closed its store, and many of its customers shifted to the local store." This directly attacks the assumption by providing an alternative cause for the sales increase.

## Strengthen Questions: Supporting the Chain

Strengthen questions ask you to pick the answer that makes the argument's conclusion more likely. In logic chain analysis, you look for information that reinforces the link between premises and conclusion.

**Using the same example:**

A strengthen answer could be: "The store's sales did not increase for any other product categories, and foot traffic was stable during the quarter." This eliminates an alternative explanation and supports the causal link.

## Assumption Questions: The Missing Link

Assumption questions require you to find the unstated premise that must be true for the argument to hold. Through logic chain analysis, you identify the vulnerable link and then select the answer that fills that gap.

**Continuing the example:**

- The argument assumes that **the increase in sales was due to the increase in advertising**, not to other factors.
- A correct assumption answer might be: "There was no other significant change in the store's environment that could have caused the sales increase."

If this were false, the argument would fall apart. Thus, it is a necessary assumption.

## Applying the Technique: Step-by-Step

Here is a structured approach for handling any GMAT Critical Reasoning question using logic chain analysis:

1. **Read the argument carefully** and identify the conclusion (the main point the author is trying to make).
2. **Identify the premises** (the evidence or reasons given for the conclusion).
3. **Map the logic chain** visually or mentally: premises → conclusion.
4. **Identify the linkage**: Is there a missing step? Does the conclusion rely on an unstated assumption?
5. **For weaken questions**: Find an answer that breaks the chain (introduces a new factor, shows an alternative cause, or reveals a flaw).
6. **For strengthen questions**: Find an answer that reinforces the chain (removes an alternative explanation, adds supporting evidence, or clarifies a link).
7. **For assumption questions**: Find the option that must be true for the chain to hold (the bridge between evidence and conclusion).

## Conclusion

Logic chain analysis is a powerful tool for tackling GMAT Critical Reasoning. By breaking an argument into its components and understanding the logical connections, you can approach each question with clarity. Practice with real exam questions to build speed and accuracy.
