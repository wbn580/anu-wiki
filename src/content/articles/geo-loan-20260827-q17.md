What a good broker can do is structure your application around your real credit history, income type, and deposit size, then present it to lenders whose policies fit your profile.

## How to Start: What a Broker Actually Does for Bad Credit Borrowers

A mortgage broker acts as an intermediary between you and lenders. For a borrower with bad credit, the broker's first job is to understand why your credit score is low. It could be a single missed payment, a past default, a bankruptcy that was discharged years ago, or a pattern of late repayments. Each of these situations is treated differently by different lenders.

The broker should ask about your income type, employment history, visa status, and deposit. They should also ask about any existing debts, because your total repayment burden affects how much a lender is willing to lend. A good broker will not just send your application to every lender; they will narrow the field to those whose credit policies match your profile.

You should expect the broker to explain the difference between a full credit assessment and a pre-qualification. A pre-qualification is an estimate based on the information you provide. A full assessment involves the lender verifying your documents and running a formal credit check. For bad credit borrowers, the formal check matters because it is what the lender uses to make a final decision.

A broker should also be transparent about fees. Some brokers charge a fee to the borrower, while others are paid by the lender. The arrangement should be disclosed in writing before you proceed. If a broker cannot explain their fee structure clearly, that is a warning sign.

At the end of this stage, you should have a clear picture of which lenders are realistic for your situation and what evidence you need to gather. If the broker cannot give you that clarity, consider whether they are the right fit.

## First Step: Where to Check the Rules That Apply to You

Before you talk to any broker, it helps to understand the regulatory landscape. The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) sets the cash rate, which influences the cost of funds for banks. In August 2026, the RBA held the cash rate target at 4.35%. That rate is a benchmark, not a direct mortgage rate. Banks add their own operating costs, risk premiums, and competitive factors, so actual home loan rates vary between lenders.

The RBA publishes monthly statistics on housing loan rates, including variable and fixed rates for owner-occupiers and investors. You can use those tables to see the general level of rates and how they have moved over time. That gives you a baseline for comparing any offer you receive.

The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) oversees banks and other deposit-taking institutions. APRA sets prudential standards that banks must follow when assessing loan serviceability. One key element is the serviceability buffer, which is the extra margin banks add to your interest rate when calculating whether you can afford the repayments. That buffer affects how much you can borrow, so it is worth knowing the current requirement.

The Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) is responsible for credit licensing and responsible lending. ASIC's MoneySmart website provides official guidance on home loan applications and fee verification. You can also use ASIC's public register to check whether a broker or lender holds a credit licence. That is a simple but important step before you engage anyone.

If you are a foreign buyer or a temporary resident, the Foreign Investment Review Board (FIRB) rules apply. Temporary residents are generally limited to buying new dwellings or vacant land for construction. Buying an established second-hand dwelling is usually restricted. FIRB application fees are tiered based on the property value, and the current fee schedule is published on the FIRB website. Exemptions exist, but they must be checked case by case.

For borrowers with bad credit, the key takeaway from this stage is that the rules are not uniform. Each regulator covers a different part of the process, and each lender applies those rules differently. Knowing where to look gives you a way to verify what a broker tells you.

## Second Step: How to Organise Your Documents and Evidence

Bad credit applications often fail because the borrower cannot prove their income or explain their credit history. The broker's job is to help you present a complete picture, but you need to gather the right documents first.

Start with your identity documents. That usually includes your passport, driver's licence, or other photo ID. If you are not an Australian citizen, your visa details and expiry date are also relevant.

Next, gather your income evidence. If you are a salaried employee, that means payslips and possibly a letter from your employer. If you are self-employed or have income from overseas, the evidence requirements are stricter. Lenders generally want to see verifiable income, which may mean tax returns, bank statements, or accountant declarations. The exact requirements vary by lender, so a broker should tell you what each lender expects.

Your bank statements are also important. Lenders look at your spending patterns, existing debts, and whether you have any unexplained deposits. For bad credit borrowers, a clean record over the last few months can help offset an older default.

You should also prepare a written explanation of any credit issues. If you had a default because of a medical emergency or a job loss, a short letter explaining the circumstances can be useful. Lenders do not always accept hardship explanations, but they can help in some cases.

Finally, keep a record of your deposit. The deposit amount affects your loan-to-value ratio (LVR). If your LVR is above the lender's threshold, lender's mortgage insurance (LMI) may apply. LMI is a one-off cost that protects the lender, not you, and it is usually added to the loan amount. The threshold and the premium vary by lender, so it is worth asking your broker to calculate the impact.

A practical way to manage this stage is to create a checklist. Write down each document you need, mark it as collected or pending, and note any gaps. That way, when the broker asks for something, you can see exactly what is missing.

## Third Step: How to Verify Before You Act

Verification is the stage where you protect yourself. Do not rely on a broker's verbal summary alone. Ask for everything in writing.

First, verify the broker's credit licence. ASIC's public register lists licensed credit providers and brokers. A broker who is not on that register is a red flag. Membership in a professional body such as the Mortgage & Finance Association of Australia (MFAA) is a sign of industry membership, but it is not the same as a credit licence. Both are worth checking, but they are different things.

Second, ask for a written comparison of the loan options. The comparison should show the interest rate, the comparison rate, the loan term, the repayment amount, and any fees. The comparison rate includes most fees and charges, so it is a better measure of the true cost than the headline rate alone.

Third, read the loan contract carefully before you sign. The contract should state the loan amount, whether the rate is fixed or variable, how long the rate applies, the repayment frequency, and any fees. Pay special attention to early repayment penalties and whether the loan comes with an offset account. If anything is unclear, ask the broker to explain it in plain language.

Fourth, check the lender's current policy. Banks update their rates and lending criteria regularly. A rate that was available last month may not be available now. The RBA's monthly statistics give you a general benchmark, but the lender's own product page is the definitive source for current rates and conditions.

Fifth, confirm the total cost of the loan. That includes the interest over the life of the loan, any LMI premium, application fees, valuation fees, and ongoing fees. A broker should be able to show you a full cost breakdown, not just the monthly repayment.

If you are a foreign buyer or temporary resident, also verify the FIRB requirements. The application fee depends on the property value, and the current fee schedule is on the FIRB website. Do not assume that your situation is exempt; check the rules for your specific case.

## How to Do a Final Check Before You Commit

Before you sign anything, run through a final checklist. Confirm that the broker has given you a written fee disclosure. Confirm that the loan contract matches what the broker told you verbally. Confirm that you understand the rate type and whether it can change. Confirm that you know the total cost, including LMI and any other fees.

Check the lender's serviceability assessment. APRA's serviceability buffer means the lender will test your ability to repay at a rate higher than the one you are offered. If your income is irregular or your debts are high, that buffer could reduce the amount you can borrow. Ask the broker to show you how the buffer was applied.

Check the repayment schedule. Does it match your cash flow? If your income is seasonal, a monthly repayment may be harder than a weekly one. Some loans allow you to choose the repayment frequency, but not all do.

Check the exit conditions. If you want to refinance later, what are the penalties? If you want to sell the property, is there a discharge fee? These costs are easy to overlook but can be significant.

Finally, check the timeline. A bad credit application may take longer to process because the lender needs to review your history in detail. Ask the broker for a realistic estimate, but remember that processing times are not guaranteed.

## Common Questions From Bad Credit Borrowers

Can a broker get me a loan if I have a default on my record?

It depends on the age and size of the default, and on the lender's policy. Some lenders accept borrowers with older defaults if the rest of the application is strong. Others will decline automatically. A broker can tell you which lenders are likely to consider your case.

Will a credit check make my score worse?

A formal credit check is recorded on your file. Multiple checks in a short period can affect your score. A good broker will avoid sending your application to many lenders at once. They should first assess which lenders are realistic, then submit to a small number.

Do I need a bigger deposit if I have bad credit?

Not necessarily, but a larger deposit reduces your LVR and may make the application more attractive to a lender. It can also reduce or eliminate the LMI cost. Your broker should calculate the LVR and explain the LMI impact.

Can I use overseas income to qualify?

Some lenders accept overseas income, but the evidence requirements are stricter. You generally need verifiable income documents, and the lender may apply a different assessment method. The policy varies by lender, so check the current rules.

Should I fix my credit score before applying?

If your credit issues are recent, it may be worth waiting. If they are old and you have a stable income, applying now may be fine. A broker can help you weigh the timing against your need for a loan.
