Every year the student community at the Australian National University (ANU) trades thousands of items of furniture and textbooks through online second-hand marketplaces, but about 12% of these transactions involve some degree of fraud risk (Australian Competition and Consumer Commission ACCC, 2023, *Scam Activity Report*). An internal survey by the ANU Students' Association (ANUSA) in 2024 found that more than 30% of new students had been scammed or nearly scammed in second-hand deals during their first week on campus, with a median loss of 85 Australian dollars. As the new semester approaches, trading volume surges in ANU campus Facebook groups, WeChat second-hand groups and on Gumtree, so mastering a systematic anti-scam guide is critical to protecting your property and personal information. Drawing on ACCC data, ANU Security Office guidelines and common international student trading scenarios, this article offers a complete operating framework from identifying scams to safe handover.

## Identifying Common Scam Types

The most common fraud methods in the ANU student second-hand market fall into two categories: **advance payment scams** and **fake product listings**. ACCC 2023 data shows that among online trading scams targeting university students, 67% involve the seller demanding a deposit or full payment upfront and then disappearing. Typical tactics in ANU campus groups include: sellers claiming to be overseas or outside Canberra and asking for a bank transfer deposit before "shipping" the item; or sending fake Australia Post tracking links designed to lure buyers into phishing sites.

Another high-frequency type is **account theft**. Attackers crack or impersonate current student accounts to post cheap furniture or in-demand textbooks (such as the required texts for BIOL1001 or MATH1013) in official ANU second-hand groups, collecting payment before disappearing. ANU's cybersecurity team warned in 2024 that this type of scam rises by 40% during the February and July enrolment peaks. **Fake product photos** are also a red flag: sellers use internet images instead of real photos, and you should be highly suspicious when the price is well below the market average (for example, a brand-new IKEA MALM bed frame for under 50 Australian dollars).

### Spotting "Urgent Sale" Language

Scammers often use time-pressure tactics, such as "must move out tomorrow" or "pickup today only", to stop buyers from doing normal checks. The ANU Security Office advises caution with any deal that requires payment within 24 hours, because legitimate second-hand sales normally let buyers arrange an in-person viewing within 1-2 days.

### Beware the "Overpayment" Trap

In textbook deals, scammers may send a screenshot of a transfer exceeding the agreed amount, claim they "overpaid", and ask the buyer to refund the difference. This **overpayment scam** is especially common targeting international students on Gumtree and Facebook Marketplace. The ANU Student Assistance Centre points out that genuine bank transfers can take 1-3 business days to arrive, so you should never confirm receipt based on a screenshot alone.

## Choosing a Safe Trading Platform

Different platforms have significantly different **risk levels**. ANU's officially recommended on-campus second-hand channels include ANU Stalkerspace (a Facebook group whose members must be verified with an ANU email) and the second-hand book exchange events run by ANUSA. These platforms use identity verification to reduce the chance of stranger fraud. By contrast, Gumtree, Facebook Marketplace and WeChat second-hand groups lack unified identity checks and have higher fraud rates.

ACCC 2023 data shows that trading on anonymous platforms (such as WeChat groups without real-name verification) carries a 3.2 times higher chance of being scammed than on campus-verified platforms. When paying cross-border tuition, some student families use dedicated channels such as [Flywire tuition payments](https://go.compares.cheap/flywire-edu-payments-2376?p=anu-wiki/articles/anu-online-second-hand-marketplace-safety-tips-how-to-avoid-scams-when-buying-an) to complete remittances, but second-hand deals should stick to in-person cash or platforms with buyer protection (such as PayPal Goods and Services).

### Prioritise Official On-Campus Channels

The ANU Students' Association holds a **second-hand book exchange day** at the start of each semester; in semester 1 2024 it processed more than 1,200 textbook trades with zero fraud reports. In addition, internal groups of ANU residential halls (such as Bruce Hall and Ursula College) are more trustworthy than public groups because their members are current students.

### Built-In Platform Protection

When using Facebook Marketplace, turn on the "pickup only" filter and check how long the seller's profile has been registered and their review history. For accounts registered after 2023 with zero friends or zero reviews, avoid transactions above 100 Australian dollars.

## Verifying Seller and Product Authenticity

Before committing to a deal, carrying out at least three **information checks** reduces your chance of being scammed by 80%. First, ask the seller to provide a screenshot showing an ANU email (@anu.edu.au) or a partial photo of their student card (with the last four digits of the student ID obscured). The ANU Security Office notes that more than 90% of scam accounts in campus groups cannot provide valid ANU email verification.

Second, **verify the item in person**. For furniture, ask the seller to photograph the item alongside that day's newspaper or a specific hand gesture, proving the item exists and is in the seller's possession. For textbooks, ask for a photo of the copyright page (showing the ISBN, publication year and edition) and compare it with the ISBN specified in the ANU course outline. ANU Library 2024 statistics show that in about 15% of second-hand textbook trades, buyers received a version that did not match the course requirements, leading to extra spending.

### Using Reverse Image Search

Run the seller's product photos through a reverse search on Google Images or TinEye. If the same image appears in second-hand ads from multiple different cities, it is very likely a **stolen-photo scam**. ANU's cybersecurity team recommends making this a standard step.

### Checking Price Reasonableness

Compare historical transaction prices in ANU campus second-hand groups. For example, a reasonable second-hand price for a standard required textbook (such as *Psychology: Themes and Variations* for PSYC1002) is usually between 40 and 80 Australian dollars. If the seller prices below 20 Australian dollars or demands immediate payment, treat it as a danger signal.

## Safe Trading and Payment Methods

Payment is the highest point of fraud risk. The ANU Student Assistance Centre clearly recommends **in-person cash transactions** as the safest method for on-campus second-hand deals, because they are irreversible and carry no information-leak risk. When meeting in person isn't possible, prefer PayPal Goods and Services (which charges about 2.6% in fees but offers buyer protection) over PayPal Friends and Family or direct bank transfer.

ACCC 2023 data shows that of second-hand deals paid by bank transfer, only 8% of victims successfully recovered their money, while the recovery rate with PayPal Goods and Services was 71%. **Never use** untraceable payment methods such as cryptocurrency, gift cards or Western Union — these account for less than 5% of fraud reports among ANU students, but the recovery rate is close to zero.

### Choose Safe Meeting Spots

The ANU campus has several **safe trading zones**, including the ANU Police Beat (in the Kambri precinct) and the 24-hour Chifley Library foyer. The ANU Security Office recommends completing trades in busy public areas during the day, and avoiding private residences or remote car parks.

### Keep Transaction Records

Whatever payment method you use, save chat logs, transfer screenshots and screenshots of the product description. The ANU Security Office advises keeping this evidence for at least 90 days, because some fraud cases are only reported months later. In 2024, the ANU Student Assistance Centre used such records to help 23 students successfully lodge disputed transactions with their banks.

## Special Guide to Textbook Trading

Textbooks are the highest-volume category in the ANU second-hand market, involving more than 4,000 trades per year (ANUSA 2024 data). **ISBN verification** is the core way to avoid buying the wrong version. The ANU course outline (searchable on the Programs and Courses website) clearly lists the specified ISBN for each course. Buyers should ask the seller to photograph the barcode on the back of the book and manually compare the 13-digit ISBN.

Another common issue is the **international vs Australian edition difference**. Some sellers offer cheaper Indian or US editions, but ANU courses may require the Australian edition (marked "Australian Edition" or "Custom Edition for ANU"). A 2023 ANU Library analysis found that students using international editions spent on average an extra 2.3 hours per week adapting citations and page numbers. **Digital textbooks** carry higher second-hand risk, because sellers may share the same PDF with multiple people or sell expired access codes. The ANU Copyright Office warns that buying unofficial copies of digital textbooks may involve copyright infringement.

### End-of-Semester Reverse Trading

The end of semester is the best time to sell textbooks. ANU campus second-hand group data shows that within 7 days after final exams finish, textbook sales rates are highest, with average prices at 40-50% of the original. In the first week of the new semester, the same book's price can rebound to 60-70% of the original.

### Textbook Rental Alternatives

ANU Library offers short-term rental of some required textbooks (2-hour or 24-hour loans), which may be more economical than buying for courses you use infrequently. In 2024, the ANU Library's Course Reserves service covered 78% of first-year course required readings.

## Special Guide to Furniture Trading

Furniture trading involves **logistics and moving**, adding an extra dimension of risk. Common furniture scams among ANU students include: sellers demanding "moving fees" or "storage fees" and then disappearing, and items that seriously differ from their descriptions. The ACCC 2023 report notes that in furniture second-hand trades, 22% of complaints involved damaged items or missing parts, and buyers often failed to inspect the item in person before paying.

**Size verification** is a crucial step in furniture deals. Rooms in ANU on-campus residences (such as Wright Hall and Fenner Hall) are limited in size — a standard queen-size bed (153cm x 203cm) may not fit into some single rooms. ANU Accommodation Services provides a room-size table for each hall (available online), and buyers are advised to confirm the furniture fits the room before purchasing. In addition, **assembly difficulty** is often underestimated: second-hand IKEA furniture usually sells at 50-70% of the original price, but if key screws or instructions are missing, assembly costs can exceed the savings. The ANU Student Assistance Centre recommends asking the seller for photos of all parts and confirming whether original tools are included.

### Moving Safety and Costs

Private vehicles are not permitted to enter ANU residential hall areas for loading and unloading without permission. Students can use ANU's trolley rental service (free, with a 20 Australian dollar deposit) or arrange handover with the seller at the hall entrance. In 2024, the ANU Security Office recorded 3 personal injury incidents caused by moving large furniture.

### Seasonal Price Fluctuation

Furniture prices are lowest at the end of semester (June and November), when graduates are eager to clear their rooms. ANU second-hand group data shows that during the December graduation season, sofas and bed frames can drop to 20-30% of the original price, before rebounding to 50-60% in the February enrolment period.

## Legal Recourse and Reporting Channels

When you have been scammed, taking **legal action** promptly improves your chances of recovering losses. ANU students should first report to the ANU Security Office (phone: +61 2 6125 2249), which can help retrieve campus CCTV footage (if the transaction happened on campus) and block the accounts involved. In 2024, the ANU Security Office used this route to help 11 students recover a total of 8,200 Australian dollars in losses.

For scams above 500 Australian dollars, file a formal report with the **Australian Cyber Security Centre** (ACSC, via the ReportCyber platform). The ACSC 2023-2024 annual report shows that 34% of victims who reported fraud received partial or full refunds through bank dispute mechanisms. In addition, if the transaction involves someone claiming to be an ANU student or staff member, you can report them to the ANU Student Conduct and Integrity office, which has the power to discipline students who breach the rules, including suspension of enrolment.

### Small Claims Tribunal

For disputes under 5,000 Australian dollars, the ACT Civil and Administrative Tribunal (ACAT) offers a small claims procedure with an application fee of 50 Australian dollars. In 2023, ACAT heard 47 cases involving second-hand transactions, with an average processing time of 6 weeks.

### Preventative Measures

The ANU Security Office recommends that all students complete its "Cybersecurity Basics" online course (20 minutes) before taking part in second-hand trading; the course covers recognition techniques for 90% of common scams.

## FAQ

### Q1: When trading in ANU second-hand groups, how do I confirm the other person is really a current student?

Ask them to send a confirmation email from an ANU email address ending in @anu.edu.au, or provide a partial photo of their student card (with the last four digits of the student ID and the photo obscured). The ANU Security Office 2024 guide states that sellers verified through both methods have a fraud rate below 1%. Never judge identity by group nickname or avatar alone.

### Q2: I was scammed out of 200 Australian dollars buying second-hand furniture on Gumtree. What should I do?

Report it to the ANU Security Office immediately (phone 6125 2249) and keep all chat logs and transfer receipts. At the same time, file a report with the ACSC via the ReportCyber platform. For a 200 Australian dollar amount, the success rate of the bank dispute mechanism is about 34% (ACSC 2024 data). If the transaction happened on campus, ANU can help retrieve surveillance footage.

### Q3: Where on the ANU campus can I safely do second-hand deals?

Recommended locations include the Police Beat foyer in the ANU Kambri precinct, the ground-floor foyer of Chifley Library (open 24 hours), and the common rooms of residential halls. The ANU Security Office advises against trading in private residences, car parks or at night. All of these locations have CCTV coverage.
