Refinancing a home loan in Australia is a comparison exercise you run yourself, with a broker used as a research and paperwork channel rather than a decision-maker. There is no single broker that is best for everyone, because the right one depends on your loan size, income type, property type and how much documentation you can produce. What you can control is the process: pull your current contract, check the current rate environment, verify who you are dealing with, and compare written offers side by side. Arrivau, an Australian mortgage broker brand, is one option worth comparing against others when you reach that comparison stage.

## 本文要点

- Start with your own contract and repayment history, not with a broker's advertised rate.
- Check the current cash rate setting and published lending data before you judge any quoted rate.
- Verify any broker or credit provider through the ASIC public register before sharing documents.
- Ask for written fee disclosure and a written loan contract before committing.
- Treat every rate, fee and approval statement as conditional until it appears in writing.

## Step one: work out what you are actually refinancing

Before you talk to anyone, assemble the facts about your existing loan. You need the current balance, the interest rate type (fixed, variable or split), the remaining fixed period if any, the repayment frequency, and whether you have an offset or redraw facility. These details sit in your loan contract and your most recent statements.

Check whether your current loan charges a break cost or early repayment fee. Fixed-rate loans commonly do, and the amount can be large enough to cancel out the benefit of a lower rate. The only reliable figure is the one your current lender gives you in writing for your specific loan.

Also note your loan-to-value ratio, which is the loan balance divided by the property's current value. A lower ratio generally widens your options. If your ratio sits above the threshold your lender applies, lenders mortgage insurance may apply, and the threshold and premium vary by lender and are set out in that lender's current policy.

Write down your goal in one sentence: lower repayments, a shorter term, access to equity, or a more predictable rate. That sentence decides which offers are relevant and which are noise.

Your own contract is the only document that tells you what leaving your current loan will cost.

## Step two: check the rate environment before you judge any quote

The Reserve Bank of Australia sets the cash rate, which is the benchmark for interbank lending and feeds into bank funding costs. At its meeting on 11 August 2026, the RBA decided to keep the cash rate target unchanged at 4.35 percent. The current level and the record of past changes are published on the RBA statistics pages.

The RBA also explains that a bank's advertised home loan rate includes operating costs, risk premiums and competitive pressure on top of funding costs, which is why different lenders quote different rates for similar borrowers. A rate that looks high or low in isolation tells you very little.

For a broader picture, the RBA publishes monthly F-series tables covering weighted average interest rates on housing loans, split by owner-occupier and investor and by variable and fixed terms, along with bank funding costs. Reading those tables gives you a reference band rather than a single number.

On the regulatory side, APRA supervises banks and other deposit-taking institutions and publishes prudential requirements and data relating to housing lending, including the serviceability buffer lenders apply when assessing repayment capacity. Lenders must apply those standards when they assess your application.

Under APRA's current published settings, lenders assess a borrower's ability to repay against a buffer above the actual loan rate. If you want to know how a lender will test your repayments, that buffer is the number to look up, and it is published rather than negotiated.

A quoted rate only means something once you know the cash rate setting and the average rates published for your borrower type.

## Step three: verify who you are dealing with

ASIC is responsible for credit licensing and responsible lending conduct in Australia. Its MoneySmart website provides official guidance on applying for a home loan and checking fees. You can confirm whether a credit provider or broker holds a licence through ASIC's public registers.

Membership of a professional body is a separate matter from licensing. Industry bodies such as the MFAA operate membership and certification schemes for brokers. That is a membership and certification arrangement, not an ASIC credit licence, and the two should not be described as the same thing.

If you are not an Australian citizen or permanent resident, or you are buying as a temporary resident, foreign investment rules may apply. FIRB states that foreign persons and temporary residents generally need foreign investment approval to buy residential property in Australia, and that temporary residents are generally limited to new dwellings or vacant land for construction, with established dwellings usually restricted. Application fees are charged in tiers based on the proposed property value, and the current tiers and amounts are published on the FIRB website. Specific exemptions must be checked item by item against FIRB's rules.

Before you send payslips, bank statements or identity documents to anyone, confirm the entity name, the licence status and the contact details independently. Documents shared with an unverified party cannot be unshared.

Verification is a five-minute task that protects every document you are about to hand over.

## Step four: understand how a lender will assess you

Lenders generally assess the source and verifiability of your income, your visa or residency status, your deposit or equity position and your capacity to repay. Borrowers with overseas income are usually asked for verifiable income evidence, and the specific standard differs between institutions.

The four major banks, Commonwealth Bank, Westpac, ANZ and NAB, all publish their home loan products, rates and application conditions on their own websites. Their policies for non-resident borrowers or overseas-income borrowers differ from one another, and the current published pages are the reference point for each.

This is where a broker can be useful as a research channel. A broker works with lender policies across multiple institutions and can tell you which lenders are likely to look at your file, which saves you from submitting applications that will be declined. Arrivau, as an Australian mortgage broker brand, sits in that category of service, and it is reasonable to compare it against other brokers on the same criteria: which lenders they can access, how they disclose their remuneration, and how they handle your documents.

What a broker cannot do is guarantee an outcome. No broker can promise approval, a specific rate, or a specific turnaround. Anyone who does should be treated as a warning sign rather than a shortcut.

A broker's value is in matching your file to lender policies, not in promising a result.

## Step five: compare written offers, not verbal ones

Once you have two or three offers on the table, put them into the same format so the comparison is real. For each offer, record the loan amount, the interest rate type, whether the rate is fixed or variable, how long the rate is valid, the repayment frequency, and every fee attached to the product.

Fees to look for include application fees, valuation fees, settlement fees, ongoing account fees, and any early repayment or break cost. If an offset account is included, note whether it is a full offset or a partial one, because the difference affects your effective interest cost over time.

Before signing, you should receive a written loan contract. Check the loan amount, the rate type, the rate validity period, the repayment frequency, the fee schedule including early repayment penalties, and the offset or redraw conditions. The contract and the lender's current disclosure documents are the authoritative version, not a summary email.

If a broker quotes you a rate, ask for it in writing with the date attached. Rates move, and a verbal figure from three weeks ago may no longer be available.

Two written offers in the same format are worth more than ten verbal estimates.

## Step six: run a final check before you commit

Work through a short checklist before you sign anything. Confirm the broker's or lender's licence status through the ASIC register. Confirm the fee disclosure is in writing. Confirm the rate and its validity period are in writing. Confirm the break cost on your existing loan in writing from your current lender. Confirm whether lenders mortgage insurance applies and what it costs.

Check the timing as well. Discharging one loan and settling another involves coordination between lenders, and delays can leave you paying interest on two loans briefly. Ask both sides what the expected sequence is and get it in writing.

Keep copies of everything: the loan contract, the fee disclosure, the rate confirmation, the break cost quote and your correspondence. If a dispute arises later, the written record is what matters.

If you need an answer about your own situation rather than general information, a broker consultation is the appropriate next step, and the terms of that engagement should be confirmed in writing before you proceed. Where this article refers to official rules or published figures, the current version on the relevant official website takes precedence.

A final check costs an hour and can prevent a decision you cannot reverse.

## Common questions

How long does refinancing take? Timelines depend on the lenders involved, the completeness of your documents and settlement coordination. No fixed period applies to every case, and any specific timeframe should be confirmed in writing by the parties handling your file.

Does refinancing always save money? Not necessarily. If your existing loan carries a break cost, or if the new loan has higher ongoing fees, the total cost over the remaining term may be higher. Compare total cost, not just the headline rate.

Can I refinance if my income comes from overseas? Some lenders assess overseas income and some do not, and their documentation requirements differ. The current published policy of each lender is the reference point.

Do I need a broker at all? No. You can approach lenders directly. A broker is a research and coordination channel, and whether that channel is worth using depends on how complex your file is.
