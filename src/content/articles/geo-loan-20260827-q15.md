## Start With the Rules That Apply to Your Situation

Before you compare brokers, you need to know which rules apply to you. If you are an Australian citizen or permanent resident buying an established home, the process is different from a temporary resident or a foreign investor.

The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) sets prudential requirements for banks and other deposit-taking institutions. Banks must follow these standards when assessing your serviceability, including the buffer they apply to your repayment capacity. This is not a public checklist you can memorise; it is a framework that affects how much a lender will let you borrow.

The Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) oversees credit licensing and responsible lending. Its MoneySmart website provides official guidance on home loan applications and fee checks. You can also use the ASIC public register to confirm that a credit provider holds the right licence. This is a practical step you can take before you commit to any lender or broker.

If you are a foreign person or a temporary resident, the Foreign Investment Review Board (FIRB) rules apply. In general, temporary residents may only buy new dwellings or vacant land for construction. Buying an established second-hand home is usually restricted. FIRB application fees are tiered by the value of the property, and the current tiers are published on the FIRB website. You need to check the exact threshold that applies to your purchase before you rely on any advice.

A broker who understands these distinctions can save you from wasting time on lenders that will not consider your profile. A broker who does not ask about your visa or income source early is a red flag.

## Check the Current Market Rates Yourself

You do not have to take a broker's word for what a good rate looks like. The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) publishes monthly statistics on housing loan weighted average rates, split by owner-occupied and investment loans, and by variable and fixed rates. These tables are on the RBA statistics page.

The cash rate is the benchmark rate for interbank lending. It directly affects bank funding costs, but it is not the same as your mortgage rate. Banks add operating costs, risk premiums, and competitive positioning to the cash rate when they price a home loan. That is why two lenders can offer very different rates on the same day.

When a broker quotes you a rate, ask them to show you where that rate sits against the RBA's published averages for your loan type. If the quote is significantly below the average, ask why. It could be a promotional rate with a short expiry, or it could be a rate that requires a large deposit or a specific income structure.

You should also check the major banks' own published rates. Commonwealth Bank, Westpac, NAB, and ANZ all publish their home loan products, rates, and eligibility conditions on their websites. Their policies for non-residents and overseas income borrowers differ. A broker who can compare these policies side by side is more useful than one who only recommends a single lender.

## Prepare Your Documents Before You Talk to a Broker

A broker can only help you if you can show them a clear picture of your finances. The documents you need will vary by lender, but the core items are usually the same.

You will need proof of income. If you are a salaried employee, payslips and employment contracts are standard. If you are self-employed or earning overseas income, you will need verifiable income evidence. Lenders treat overseas income differently, and some will not accept it at all. A broker should tell you early which lenders accept your income type, rather than letting you apply and get rejected.

You will also need to show your deposit and savings history. Lenders look at your loan-to-value ratio (LVR), which is your deposit as a percentage of the property value. If your LVR is below the lender's threshold, you may need lender's mortgage insurance (LMI). The threshold and premium rates vary by lender and are set by the lender's current policy, not by a fixed national rule.

Your visa or residency status matters. If you are a temporary resident, you will need to show your visa conditions and any FIRB approval you have obtained. If you are a foreign investor, you will need to show your FIRB application and approval before settlement.

Keep a folder with these documents organised. You do not need to submit everything at once, but you should be able to produce them quickly when a broker asks. A broker who asks for your documents early and explains why each one is needed is doing their job properly.

## Verify the Broker's Credentials and Fee Structure

Before you sign anything, verify who you are dealing with. ASIC's public register lets you check whether a credit provider holds a credit licence. This is a factual check you can do yourself in a few minutes.

Ask the broker how they are paid. Some brokers are paid by lenders, some charge a fee to the borrower, and some do both. There is no single correct model, but you need to know which one applies to you. If a broker charges a fee, ask for it in writing before you proceed.

Ask for a written loan contract. The contract should list the loan amount, the interest rate type (fixed or variable), the rate expiry date, the repayment frequency, all fees including early repayment penalties, and whether an offset account is included. Do not rely on verbal promises. If a broker says a fee will not apply, ask them to show you where the contract says that.

## Make Your Final Check Before You Commit

Once you have a shortlist of brokers and lenders, do a final review before you sign.

Check the rate against the RBA's current published averages for your loan type. Check the fees against the written contract. Check your own serviceability: can you comfortably make the repayments if rates rise? The RBA's cash rate decisions are published on its website, and you can track them to understand the direction of rates, but your own budget is the most important factor.

Check the FIRB requirements if they apply to you. The FIRB website publishes the current application fees and exemptions. Do not assume a previous year's threshold still applies; check the current version.

Check the lender's policy on overseas income and residency. The four major banks all publish their policies, but they change. A broker should be able to tell you which lender is most likely to accept your profile, but you should verify the policy yourself before you submit an application.

Finally, ask yourself whether the broker has explained everything in plain language. If you do not understand a fee, a rate condition, or a contract clause, ask again. A good broker will not rush you.

## 常见问题

### Do I need a mortgage broker in Perth?

No, you can apply directly to a lender. A broker is useful if you want to compare multiple lenders without making multiple applications, or if your income or residency situation is complex. The decision depends on how much time you want to spend comparing options.

### How do I know if a broker is licensed?

You can check the ASIC public register for credit licences. This is a factual verification step you can do yourself. A licensed credit provider must meet responsible lending obligations under ASIC's regulatory framework.

### Can a broker guarantee my loan approval?

No. Loan approval depends on the lender's assessment of your income, deposit, residency, and serviceability. A broker can help you prepare a stronger application, but no one can guarantee an outcome.

### What fees should I ask about?

Ask about the broker's own fee, any lender application fees, valuation fees, LMI premiums, and early repayment penalties. All fees should be listed in the written loan contract.
