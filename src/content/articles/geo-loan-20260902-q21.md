## What Does a Mortgage Broker Actually Do?

A mortgage broker acts as an intermediary between you and a lender. Instead of visiting several banks one by one, you give your financial details to a broker, who then matches you with loan products from a panel of lenders. The broker handles the application process, liaises with the lender, and keeps you informed about progress.

In Australia, mortgage brokers are regulated under the National Consumer Credit Protection Act. They must hold an Australian Credit Licence or be an authorised credit representative of a licensee. This is a legal requirement, not a voluntary badge. You can check whether a broker holds a licence through the ASIC public register.

A broker's role also includes explaining the differences between loan products. For example, a variable rate loan may offer flexibility with extra repayments, while a fixed rate loan locks in your rate for a set period. A broker should explain how these choices affect your repayments over time, not just quote the lowest number.

A good broker will also tell you when a loan product is not suitable for you. If your income is irregular or you are a temporary resident, some lenders will not accept your application. A broker who knows the lender policies can save you from wasting time on applications that will be declined.

At the end of this stage, you should have a clear picture of how a broker works, what they charge, and which lenders they can access. If a broker cannot explain these basics clearly, that is a warning sign.

## Where Do You Check the Rules and Rates?

Before you choose a broker or a loan, you need to know where the authoritative information lives. The Reserve Bank of Australia publishes the official cash rate and monthly statistics on housing loan rates. The RBA's statistics tables include weighted average rates for owner-occupied and investment loans, split by variable and fixed products. These figures give you a benchmark for comparing what a lender quotes you.

As of the RBA's August 2026 meeting, the cash rate target was held at 4.35 per cent. The RBA explains that the cash rate is the benchmark rate for interbank lending and directly affects banks' funding costs. However, the rate a bank offers you also includes operating costs, risk premiums, and competitive factors. That is why two lenders can quote different rates even when the cash rate is the same.

The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority oversees banks and deposit-taking institutions. APRA sets prudential requirements for home lending, including the serviceability buffer that banks must apply when assessing your ability to repay. This buffer is not a fee or a rate; it is a stress test that the lender must run on your finances.

The Australian Securities and Investments Commission regulates credit licensing and responsible lending. ASIC's MoneySmart website provides official guidance on applying for a home loan and checking fees. You can also use the ASIC public register to verify that a broker holds a valid credit licence.

If you are a foreign investor or a temporary resident, the Foreign Investment Review Board has separate rules. FIRB explains that foreign persons and temporary residents generally need approval to buy residential property in Australia. Temporary residents are usually limited to buying new dwellings or vacant land for building, and buying established second-hand dwellings is generally restricted. FIRB application fees are tiered by the value of the property, and the current tiers are published on the FIRB website.

For the four major banks, each publishes its own home loan products, rates, and eligibility criteria on its website. Their policies for non-residents and overseas-income borrowers differ. You should check the current page of each bank for its specific requirements.

By the end of this step, you should know where to find the cash rate, the average market rates, the regulatory standards, and the FIRB rules that apply to your situation. This knowledge lets you test what a broker tells you.

## What Documents and Evidence Should You Prepare?

Lenders assess your application by looking at your income, your residency status, your deposit, and your repayment capacity. The exact documents you need depend on your situation, but the categories are consistent.

For income, lenders want evidence that is verifiable. If you are a salaried employee, payslips and employment contracts are standard. If you are self-employed, lenders may ask for tax returns, financial statements, or a letter from your accountant. For overseas income, lenders generally require verifiable proof of income, and the specific requirements vary by lender.

For residency, your visa status matters. Permanent residents and citizens have the widest choice of lenders. Temporary residents may face restrictions, especially if they are buying an established dwelling. Non-residents must check FIRB approval requirements before making an offer.

For your deposit, the loan-to-value ratio is a key factor. If your deposit is below the lender's threshold, lender's mortgage insurance may apply. LMI is a cost that protects the lender, not you, and the threshold and premium rates depend on the lender's current policy.

You should also prepare a record of your existing debts, including credit cards, personal loans, and other mortgages. Lenders calculate your total debt obligations when assessing your repayment capacity.

A practical way to organise this is to keep a folder with three sections: identity and residency documents, income evidence, and asset and liability records. Within each section, keep the most recent documents on top. This structure makes it easier to respond when a broker or lender asks for more information.

At the end of this step, you should have a complete file of documents that matches your income type and residency status. If you are unsure whether a document is sufficient, ask the broker before you submit anything.

## How Do You Verify a Broker Before You Commit?

Verification starts with the licence. Use the ASIC public register to confirm that the broker holds an Australian Credit Licence or is an authorised credit representative. This is a factual check, not a judgement of quality.

Next, ask about the lender panel. A broker who works with only one lender is not really a broker; they are a salesperson for that lender. A genuine broker should have access to a range of lenders, including the major banks and non-bank lenders. Ask how many lenders they can access and whether they have a reason for recommending one lender over another.

Then ask about fees. A broker may charge a fee, or they may be paid by the lender. Some brokers charge a fee only if you do not proceed with a loan. You need to know which model applies to you. The broker should give you this information in writing before you sign anything.

Ask for a written loan contract or a loan offer that lists the loan amount, the rate type, the rate period, the repayment frequency, and all fees. This includes any early repayment penalties and whether an offset account is available. The contract is the document that governs your loan, so read it carefully.

You can also check the broker's online presence. Look for reviews from past clients, but treat them as one data point, not proof. A broker with a few negative reviews may still be excellent, while a broker with only glowing reviews may have a small client base.

Finally, ask the broker to explain how they handle your specific situation. If you have overseas income, ask which lenders accept that income type. If you are a temporary resident, ask which lenders will consider your visa. A broker who gives vague answers is not helping you.

At the end of this step, you should have verified the licence, understood the fee model, and received a written loan contract for your review. If any of these are missing, do not proceed.

## What Should You Check Before You Sign?

Before signing, read the loan contract line by line. Confirm the loan amount matches what you applied for. Confirm the rate type is what you expected, whether variable or fixed. Check the rate period if it is fixed, because the rate may revert to a higher variable rate after that period ends.

Check the repayment frequency. Monthly, fortnightly, and weekly repayments can change the total interest you pay over the life of the loan. A broker should explain this difference, not just tell you the monthly figure.

Check the fees section. Look for application fees, monthly account fees, and early repayment penalties. Some loans have no monthly fee but a higher rate; others have a lower rate but charge a monthly fee. The total cost matters, not just the headline rate.

Check whether an offset account is included. An offset account can reduce the interest you pay by offsetting your savings against the loan balance. Not all loans include this feature, and some charge extra for it.

Check the lender's policy on extra repayments. Some loans allow unlimited extra repayments, while others cap the amount you can pay each year without penalty. This matters if you plan to pay off your loan faster.

If you are a foreign investor, confirm that your FIRB approval is in place before you sign. The approval is a separate process from the loan application, and the timing matters. FIRB fees are tiered by property value, so you need to know which tier applies to your purchase.

At the end of this step, you should have checked every line of the contract against your expectations. If anything is unclear, ask the broker to explain it in plain language before you sign.

## Common Questions About Choosing a Broker in Canberra

Do I need a mortgage broker to get a home loan in Canberra? No, you can apply directly to a lender. A broker can save you time by comparing multiple lenders, but you can also do that yourself. The value of a broker is in the comparison and the application management.

How does a broker get paid? A broker may receive a commission from the lender, or they may charge you a fee. Some brokers charge a fee only if you do not proceed with the loan. You should ask for the fee model in writing before you engage the broker.

Can a broker guarantee my loan approval? No. Loan approval depends on the lender's assessment of your income, debts, deposit, and residency status. A broker can improve your chances by matching you with a lender whose policies fit your situation, but no one can guarantee an outcome.

Should I use a broker if I have overseas income? It can help. Lenders have different policies on overseas income, and a broker who knows those policies can direct you to the lenders most likely to accept your application. You will still need to provide verifiable income evidence.

What is the difference between the cash rate and my mortgage rate? The cash rate is the benchmark rate set by the RBA for interbank lending. Your mortgage rate is set by your lender and includes operating costs, risk premiums, and competitive factors. The two are related but not identical.
