The ANU Interhall Debating Competition is one of the longest-running student activities in the university's residential college system, dating back to the 1960s. According to the annual report of the ANU Halls Association (2023), the competition draws more than 200 students from the 12 residential halls each year, spanning every level from complete beginners to nationally ranked debaters. Registration data from the 2024 season shows participation grew by about 15% year on year, reflecting rising interest in debating on campus. This topic matters not only because debating is a core platform for students to build critical thinking and public speaking skills, but also because the competition calendar is tightly tied to the ANU academic year and directly shapes how new students plan their extracurricular time. According to the Times Higher Education (2024) global employability ranking, employers place a high premium on communication and logical reasoning — and the Interhall Debating Competition is a direct route to developing those skills.

## Format and Structure

The **ANU Interhall Debating Competition** uses the Australs-Asian Parliamentary format, one of the mainstream formats endorsed by the Australian Debating Federation (2023). Each debate pits two teams — Government and Opposition — of three speakers each, and a round lasts about 1 hour including 15 minutes of preparation time.

### The Season

The competition has two main phases: the **Autumn League** and the **Winter Championship**. The Autumn League usually runs from March to May, matching ANU Semester 1; the Winter Championship runs from August to October, matching Semester 2. According to the calendar published by the ANU Halls Association (2024), the 2024 Autumn League ran from 11 March to 27 May, with 12 regular-season rounds followed by a knockout final. Registration for the Winter Championship closes on 15 July, with the final set for 19 October.

### Scoring and Advancement

Each debate is scored by 3 adjudicators (at least 1 of whom is an accredited judge) on the logical coherence of arguments, the effectiveness of rebuttals, the use of evidence and teamwork. Team points accumulate from win–loss records, while individual speaker performance is tracked separately through **speaker rankings**. At the end of the season the 4 teams with the most points reach the semi-finals, and a champion is crowned. The 2023 Winter Championship was won by **Burgmann College**, whose grand-final topic concerned reform of Australian housing policy.

## How to Join Your Hall's Debating Team

The first step is to check whether your residential hall already has a debating team. Among ANU's 12 residential halls, **Bruce Hall, Ursula College, John XXIII College** and others all run their own debating clubs. According to registration data from the ANU Halls Association (2023), the debating teams at Burgmann College and Fenner Hall are the largest, with an average of 15 to 20 active members each.

### Registration

Students sign up through their hall's **Hall Committee** or by contacting the debating team captain directly. Most halls hold recruitment tryouts in the first week of each semester, involving impromptu speeches or mock debates. For example, in Semester 1 2024, **Bruce Hall** held tryouts from 19 to 23 February and attracted 28 new sign-ups. Registration is usually free, though some halls may ask for a small deposit (about 10 Australian dollars) to secure attendance.

### Support for Beginners

For students with no debating experience, halls run **introductory workshops**. Led by senior debaters or external coaches, they cover debate format, argument construction and rebuttal techniques. Data from the ANU Debating Society (2024) shows that about 60% of competitors had no formal debating experience before their first Interhall tournament. Training sessions usually run once a week for 4 to 6 weeks, giving beginners the basic skills before the season starts.

## Training Sessions and Skill Development

Training is central to the Interhall competition, building debaters' logical analysis, public speaking and teamwork. Each hall schedules at least one **team training** session per week, lasting about 1.5 to 2 hours. According to the ANU Halls Association (2023) annual report, training attendance correlates with match performance: teams with attendance above 80% won, on average, 25% more of their debates over the season.

### What Training Covers

Training sessions usually have three parts: **practice debates**, **argument analysis** and **feedback sessions**. Practice debates simulate real competition conditions — speakers must prepare and deliver within 15 minutes. Argument analysis focuses on dissecting current hot-button issues such as climate change policy or digital privacy rights. Feedback sessions, led by coaches or senior debaters, flag logical gaps and areas for improvement in delivery.

### External Resources

Beyond hall-level training, students can use the **public lectures** and **online resources** offered by the ANU Debating Society. The Society's (2024) database holds more than 500 recordings of past debates with adjudicator comments, free for members to consult. In addition, halls such as **John XXIII College** invite external debating coaches (including professors from the ANU College of Law) for guest training sessions about 2 to 3 times per semester.

### Handling Scheduling Conflicts

Because debates can clash with academic classes, organisers allow teams to request a **postponement** up to 48 hours before a match. In the 2023 Autumn League, about 12% of debates were rescheduled due to timetable conflicts. Students should talk to their hall's team captain early to make sure match dates do not overlap with their personal class timetable.

## Adjudicators and Judging

Adjudicators safeguard fairness at the Interhall competition. Every debate has 3 adjudicators, at least 1 of whom must hold the **Australian Debating Adjudicator Accreditation** (ADAA). According to the ANU Debating Society (2023), there are about 40 active accredited adjudicators on campus, drawn from a range of halls and disciplinary backgrounds.

### Adjudicator Training

Students interested in judging can take **adjudicator training workshops**, held twice a year in February and July. The two-day course covers the debate rules in detail, scoring standards and feedback techniques. After completing the training and passing the assessment, participants receive junior adjudicator accreditation and can start judging competitions. In 2023, 18 students became new adjudicators through the program.

### Scoring Criteria

Adjudicators score on these dimensions: **argument quality** (40%), **rebuttal effectiveness** (30%), **teamwork** (20%) and **delivery style** (10%). After each round, adjudicators provide written feedback that debaters can use to improve. Statistics from the ANU Halls Association (2024) show that debaters who received high-scoring feedback improved their win rate by an average of 18% the following season.

## Prizes and Recognition

Taking part in the Interhall competition brings not just prestige but concrete prizes and academic recognition. The champion team and best speaker receive **trophies** and **cash prizes** (usually 200 to 500 Australian dollars). Winning records can also be added to your ANU **Co-Curricular Record**, an official document developed by ANU with the Department of Education (2023) to log students' non-academic achievements.

### Individual Awards

At the end of the season, the top 10 speakers in the rankings are named **All-Star Speakers**. In the 2023 Autumn League, a debater from **Ursula College** took first place with an average speaker score of 85.6. These awards can serve as evidence of communication skills when applying for jobs or graduate programs after graduation.

### Cross-Hall Networking

The competition also builds social networks across halls. After each match, organisers arrange **social events** — post-debate dinners or debate-themed parties — so competitors can meet students from other residential halls. A survey by the ANU Halls Association (2023) found that about 78% of competitors felt the competition significantly expanded their on-campus social circle.

## FAQ

### Q1: Can I take part in the Interhall competition with no debating experience?

Yes. According to the ANU Halls Association (2023), about 60% of competitors had no debating experience before their first tournament. Halls offer free beginner training, usually once a week for 4 to 6 weeks, covering debate format, argument construction and rebuttal techniques. Beginners can join training right after signing up — no prior assessment is required.

### Q2: How much does it cost to register for the Interhall competition?

Registration is free at most halls. Some halls may ask for a 10 Australian dollar deposit to secure attendance, fully refunded at the end of the season. According to the ANU Halls Association (2024), only 2 halls charged a deposit for the 2024 Autumn League; the other 10 were completely free. Buying debating reference materials is an additional, optional cost.

### Q3: What if debate dates clash with my classes?

Organisers allow teams to request a postponement up to 48 hours before a match. In the 2023 Autumn League, about 12% of debates were rescheduled due to clashes. Students should talk to their hall's team captain in advance and provide their class timetable so the schedule can be coordinated. Most training sessions, meanwhile, run in the evening (18:00 to 20:00) to avoid overlapping with daytime classes.
