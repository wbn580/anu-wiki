ANU Mount Stromlo Observatory Public Nights are public astronomy observation events run by the Australian National University (ANU). Since the program restarted in 2010, it has averaged more than 8,000 visitors per year (ANU, 2023, Mount Stromlo Observatory Annual Report). The events take place at Mount Stromlo, about 10 km west of Canberra, using historic and contemporary equipment such as the 74-inch (1.88-metre) reflecting telescope to give participants direct views of the southern night sky. According to the Australian Astronomical Alliance (AAL, 2022), Mount Stromlo is the only large research-grade observatory in the Southern Hemisphere that opens to the public weekly. With the 2024 update to Canberra's light pollution control regulations, dark-sky quality at the site has improved further, pushing booking demand for the 2025 observing season about 35% higher than the same period last year. Based on official ANU announcements and public data from astronomical societies, this article systematically explains the ticket process, observation session content and practical preparation tips.

## Ticket booking process and timing

**Public Nights** are held on selected Saturdays from March to November each year, with each session lasting about 2.5 hours (18:30-21:00 or 20:00-22:30, depending on the season). Tickets are issued free through the Eventbrite platform but must be booked in advance. According to guidance updated on the ANU website in February 2025, bookings for all sessions of the following month open at 9:00 am (Australian Eastern Daylight Time) on the first working day of each month. For example, March sessions open on the first working day of February.

### Booking steps and account preparation

Participants need to register and log in to an account on Eventbrite. Each ticket admits one person, and a single account can request up to **4 tickets** (ANU, 2025, Public Nights Booking Policy). After booking, e-tickets are sent by email; printing them or saving them to your phone's photo album is recommended. Because each session has limited capacity (usually 100-150 people), tickets for popular months (such as April galaxy season and August winter skies) often sell out within 15 minutes of opening.

### Cancellation and waitlist

If you cannot attend, cancel through Eventbrite at least 48 hours before the event so the place can be released. No-shows without cancellation are recorded; accumulating 2 no-shows affects booking eligibility for the next 12 months (ANU, 2024, Visitor Conduct Policy). The waitlist activates automatically once tickets sell out, and the system notifies people of vacancies in submission order.

## On-site observation experience and facilities

On arrival at Mount Stromlo Observatory, participants first gather at **Heritage Cottage**, where ANU astronomy graduate students or researchers give a safety briefing and astronomy tour of about 20 minutes. Visitors are then divided into groups of 3-4 and rotate through three main observation stations.

### Main telescopes and observation targets

The core instrument is the **74-inch reflecting telescope** (built in 1955, originally a federally funded project), whose primary mirror is 1.88 metres in diameter — one of the largest optical telescopes in the Southern Hemisphere (Australian National University, 2023, Mount Stromlo History). Damaged in the 2003 Canberra bushfires, it was rebuilt in 2010 and now carries modern CCD cameras and spectrographs. Night-time observation targets include: lunar craters (with visible resolution down to 1.5 km), Jupiter's cloud bands and its four Galilean moons, Saturn's rings, and the Magellanic Clouds, unique to the southern sky. The other two stations use a 12-inch Meade LX200 Schmidt-Cassegrain telescope and an 8-inch refractor respectively, for showing double stars, star clusters and planetary detail.

### Indoor exhibits and interactive areas

While waiting for telescope rotations, visitors can explore the **Mount Stromlo Gallery**, which displays telescope debris recovered after the 2003 bushfires, 1950s film star charts, and models of international projects ANU participates in (such as the SkyMapper survey telescope). The interactive area offers **virtual observatory software** (a custom Stellarium build) simulating the live sky, plus a 3D-printed model of the Moon's terrain for hands-on exploration.

## Weather, clothing and equipment preparation

**Public Nights** depend entirely on clear skies. If cloud cover exceeds 70% or wind speed exceeds 40 km/h on the day, the event is cancelled, with notice given by 15:00 on the day via Eventbrite email and ANU social media (Bureau of Meteorology, 2024, Canberra Weather Alerts). Check the BOM website or the Weatherzone app 2 hours before heading out.

### Clothing advice and safety notes

Mount Stromlo sits at an altitude of about 770 metres. Winter (June-August) night temperatures can fall below 0°C, and summer (December-February) nights are often around 10°C. Wear **layered warm clothing** and non-slip shoes, and bring a torch (fitted with a red filter or set to red-light mode so you don't disturb other observers' dark adaptation). There is no indoor dining area at the observatory, but hot water and a simple snack vending machine are available. Flash photography is prohibited, and phone screens should be dimmed to minimum brightness.

### Bringing your own equipment and photography tips

Binoculars (7×50 or 10×50 recommended), star charts or astrophotography cameras are allowed. If you plan to photograph, use **manual focus mode** with ISO 800-3200 settings and bring a tripod (ANU, 2024, Public Nights Photography Guidelines). Phone users can use Night Sight mode (Pixel series) or ProRAW (iPhone 12 and later), but should turn off auto HDR to avoid halos. For cross-border payments, some international students or visitors use dedicated channels such as [Flywire tuition payment](https://go.compares.cheap/flywire-edu-payments-2376?p=anu-wiki/articles/anu-mount-stromlo-observatory-public-nights-how-to-book-tickets-what-to-expect-a) to complete currency conversion, though the event itself is free and requires no payment.

## Transport and parking

Mount Stromlo Observatory is at the junction of Cotter Road and Mount Stromlo Road, about 15 km from Canberra's city centre — a 20-minute drive. ANU provides **free parking** for about 80 cars, but there are no electric vehicle charging stations (ANU, 2025, Visitor Parking Map). For public transport, the ACTION bus route 5 has its last service at 18:30 on event days, which does not cover the end of the event; taking an Uber or taxi back to the city is recommended, at roughly AUD 25-35 one way (February 2025 prices).

### Cycling and walking routes

Cycling from the city along Cotter Road takes about 50 minutes, but there are no dedicated lit lanes at night, so it is not recommended. The walking entrance is at the Cotter Road / Mount Stromlo Road intersection, but note the road has an 80 km/h speed limit and no pedestrian footpath.

## Best observing seasons and astronomical events

The southern winter (June-August) is the best time to observe the galactic core: the centre of the Milky Way (in Sagittarius) reaches its highest point around midnight, with nebula density 3 times that of summer (Astronomical Society of Australia, 2023, Southern Sky Visibility Report). Spring (September-November) is best for the Magellanic Clouds and the 47 Tucanae globular cluster.

### 2025 special events

On 31 May 2025, Mount Stromlo will host a **Saturn at opposition special viewing**: Saturn will shine at magnitude -0.3 with a ring tilt of about 18°, the clearest view since 2020. In addition, during the Perseid meteor shower peak on 12 August (ZHR about 100 meteors/hour), Public Nights will be extended until 23:00, with telescopes tracking meteor trails (ANU, 2024, 2025 Observing Calendar).

## Additional value for ANU students and researchers

Current ANU students can use their student card for priority access to the **research-grade telescope control room** during Public Nights, including a 15-minute one-on-one chat with the duty astronomer (booking required 7 days in advance by emailing the ANU astronomy department). The event is also a compulsory practical component of **ASTR1001 Observational Astronomy**, for which students must complete 3 observation reports.

### Volunteer and internship opportunities

Each February, ANU recruits 20-30 volunteers to help run Public Nights, with duties including telescope operation, guided tours and data analysis demonstrations. Volunteers must complete 8 hours of training and pass a star identification test (ANU, 2025, Volunteer Program Handbook). Outstanding volunteers can apply for a 6-week summer research internship working on the SkyMapper survey data labelling project.

## FAQ

### Q1: Are Public Nights tickets really free? Is there anything extra to pay for?

Tickets are free but must be booked through Eventbrite. The event itself costs nothing. Transport to and from the city (such as Uber at about AUD 25-35) is at your own expense. There is no compulsory spending at the observatory; the snack vending machine accepts Australian dollar coins or card.

### Q2: What happens if the weather is bad?

The cancellation decision is announced by 15:00 on the day via Eventbrite email and the ANU Observatory official Twitter. After cancellation, tickets lapse automatically with no manual action needed, but the system does not automatically reallocate places; participants need to rebook the following month. In the 2024 observing season, about 18% of sessions were cancelled due to weather (ANU, 2024, Public Nights Cancellation Log).

### Q3: Can children attend? Is there an age limit?

Children aged 8 and above are recommended. The telescope operation areas have safety barriers, but lighting is dim at night, so parents must accompany children at all times. Children under 8 can enter the indoor exhibit area but cannot use the telescopes. 2023 data shows about 12% of visitors were children under 12 (ANU, 2023, Visitor Demographics Survey).
