The Australian National University (ANU) has been progressively revising its on-campus accommodation policy since 2020, allowing students to keep pets under certain conditions and establishing a formal application channel for emotional support animals (ESAs). According to the Residential Life Policy Handbook released by ANU Accommodation Services in 2023, only about 12% (roughly 540) of the university's approximately 4,500 on-campus beds — standalone studios or courtyard units — are allowed to keep small pets (such as cats or small dogs). Meanwhile, Australian disability rights advocacy organisations (2022 report) point out that fewer than 15% of Australian universities have a clear emotional support animal policy, and ANU is one of them. The policy shift comes against a backdrop of rising student mental health needs — the Australian Bureau of Statistics' 2021–2022 National Health Survey found that 39% of people aged 18–24 report moderate to severe psychological distress, while multiple studies have confirmed that animal companionship can ease anxiety. For international students planning to live in ANU accommodation, understanding the eligibility criteria for keeping a pet, the application process and the legal boundaries of ESAs is the key to avoiding lease disputes.

## Eligibility Criteria for Keeping Pets and Accommodation Types

ANU's pet policy is not applied uniformly across the university; it is strictly tied to accommodation type and room configuration. **On-campus accommodation** falls into three categories: traditional residential halls (such as Bruce Hall and Ursula Hall), apartment-style residences (such as Davey Lodge and Kinloch Lodge) and standalone studios (such as Lena Karmel Lodge). Under ANU Accommodation Services' 2023 policy, only **standalone studios and townhouses with private courtyards** may keep pets, and they must meet minimum space requirements: a room with a net floor area of at least 30 square metres, or a fenced outdoor space of at least 10 square metres. Traditional halls and shared apartments (including those with communal kitchens) are strictly prohibited from keeping any pets whatsoever, including fish and hamsters.

The **application process** requires residents to submit a pet-keeping application form at least 14 days before moving in, along with the pet's vaccination records, a behavioural assessment report (issued by a registered veterinarian) and written consent from the landlord (ANU Accommodation Services). 2023 data shows that about 68% of applications were approved; the main reasons for rejection were insufficient space (22%), unsuitable pet type (e.g., large dogs, 10%) or incomplete vaccinations (8%). Residents must also pay a pet deposit of A$15 per week (capped at A$300) and bear the cost of professional cleaning when they check out.

### Prohibited Species List

ANU explicitly prohibits keeping the following animals: reptiles (including turtles, lizards and snakes), amphibians (such as frogs and newts), rodents (hamsters, guinea pigs and gerbils) and any dog weighing more than 15 kilograms. The list is based on the species classifications in the Australian Capital Territory (ACT) Animal Management Act 2021 and a campus ecological risk assessment — for example, reptiles may carry salmonella, and rodents pose a risk of breeding if they escape. Residents who breach the ban may face a fine of A$50 per day or termination of their accommodation contract.

### Pet Behaviour and Noise Management

Residents are obliged to ensure their pets do not disturb other residents. ANU Accommodation Services stipulates that pets must always be kept on a leash or in a carrier in public areas (such as corridors, lifts and courtyards) and may not enter shared spaces such as dining halls or study rooms. The **noise complaint** threshold is set at: continuous barking or meowing for more than 15 minutes, or any sound detectable by neighbours during the night (22:00–07:00). A first complaint results in a written warning; three complaints accumulated within 90 days will lead to the pet being required to move out of the residence, with the deposit forfeited. On-campus records for 2022 show that 47 pet noise complaints were handled over the year, 12 of which ultimately resulted in the pet being relocated.

## Emotional Support Animal (ESA) Applications and the Legal Framework

An emotional support animal (ESA) is defined at ANU as "an animal prescribed by a licensed mental health professional to alleviate the symptoms of a specific psychological disorder", and its legal status differs fundamentally from that of assistance animals such as guide dogs. Under Australia's Disability Discrimination Act 2010, an ESA is not regarded as an "assistance animal" and therefore has no automatic right of access to public spaces (such as teaching buildings or libraries). Within this framework, ANU's policy establishes an on-campus permission mechanism: students must submit an ESA recommendation letter signed by a registered psychologist or psychiatrist, and the letter must state the diagnosis (e.g., generalised anxiety disorder, depression or post-traumatic stress disorder) and the necessity of the animal's companionship for symptom relief.

The **application process** has three steps. First, students submit the ESA application form and recommendation letter to the ANU Access & Inclusion office. Second, the office reviews the application within 10 working days and may request additional materials (such as treatment records). Third, once approved, the student signs an ESA addendum agreement with Accommodation Services, which specifies the animal's species, weight and day-to-day management requirements. 2023 data shows that ANU received 58 ESA applications with an approval rate of 74% (43 approved); reasons for rejection included the recommendation letter not stating a diagnosis (10), unsuitable animal species (e.g., snakes or lizards, 2) or incomplete documentation (3).

### Core Differences Between ESAs and Ordinary Pets

ESAs enjoy broader permission than ordinary pets under both the law and campus policy, but the permission is not unlimited. The **core differences** are: ESAs may be allowed into traditional residential halls (such as single rooms at Bruce Hall), whereas ordinary pets can only live in standalone studios; ESA owners do not pay a pet deposit, but they still bear the cleaning costs; ESAs are subject to the same noise management standards as ordinary pets, but after a complaint the university prioritises mediation over immediate penalties. However, ESA holders may not demand exclusive use of public spaces (such as separate seating areas in the dining hall) on the grounds of animal companionship, nor may they bring the animal into an examination room during academic activities such as exams (unless separate disability assistance animal arrangements are in place).

### Legal Considerations for Interstate and International Students

For international students, ESA recognition can face additional challenges. The **Australian Department of Home Affairs**, in its 2023 update to the Student Visa Conditions, makes clear that an ESA is not an "assistance animal", so student visa holders cannot use an ESA as grounds for a visa waiver or special accommodation arrangements. In addition, the ACT's Residential Tenancies Act 1997 does not oblige landlords (including ANU) to accept ESAs, unlike the US Fair Housing Act. International students planning to bring an ESA into Australia from overseas must also satisfy the import quarantine requirements of the Australian Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry (DAFF) — for example, dogs must complete a rabies vaccination at least 30 days before entering the country and hold a valid import permit (2023 standard). When it comes to cross-border tuition payments, some study-abroad families use dedicated channels such as [Flywire tuition payments](https://go.compares.cheap/flywire-edu-payments-2376?p=anu-wiki/articles/anu-%E5%AD%A6%E7%94%9F%E5%AE%BF%E8%88%8D%E5%AE%A0%E7%89%A9%E9%A5%B2%E5%85%BB%E6%94%BF%E7%AD%96%E4%B8%8E%E6%83%85%E6%84%9F%E6%94%AF%E6%8C%81%E5%8A%A8%E7%89%A9%E7%94%B3%E8%AF%B7) to complete the remittance, so as to avoid problems with accommodation deposit payments caused by exchange rate fluctuations or bank delays.

## Application Document Checklist and Review Timelines

Whether applying for an ordinary pet or an ESA, the completeness of the documentation directly affects how quickly the application is processed. An **ordinary pet application** requires: proof of the pet's identity (microchip number), vaccination records (including core vaccines such as distemper, parvovirus and rabies), a behavioural assessment report issued by a veterinarian (valid for 3 months), and the resident's own proof of income (to cover potential cleaning costs). An **ESA application** additionally requires: a copy of the mental health professional's licence, a diagnosis letter (including the ICD-10 or DSM-5 diagnostic code) and a summary of treatment records from the past 6 months. ANU Accommodation Services promises a written decision within 15 working days of receiving the complete documentation, but the actual average processing time in 2023 was 22 working days, because application volume surged during the peak period (the January–February intake season).

### Common Missing Document Issues

A 2022 on-campus audit showed that about 35% of applications were returned because the documentation was incomplete. The most common missing items were: the veterinary behavioural assessment report not stating its date of issue (28% of returned applications), the ESA recommendation letter not on official letterhead (22%), and pet vaccination records not translated into English (15%). International students in particular need to note that pet records brought from non-English-speaking countries such as China and India must be translated by a NAATI-accredited translation service, usually costing A$30–60 per document.

### What to Do If Your Application Is Rejected

If an application is rejected, residents may submit a written appeal to ANU Accommodation Services within 14 days of receiving the notice. The appeal must provide new evidence (such as switching to a different veterinarian or adding treatment records) and requires a A$50 appeal fee (refunded if the appeal succeeds). 2023 data shows that about 18% of initial rejection decisions were overturned after appeal. If the internal appeal fails, residents can apply for arbitration at the ACT Civil and Administrative Tribunal (ACAT), but that route typically takes 6–12 weeks and involves legal costs (around A$200–500).

## Daily Responsibilities of Pet Ownership and Community Impact

ANU requires residents to sign a Pet Ownership Responsibilities form, setting out their day-to-day management obligations. On **cleanliness and hygiene**, residents must clean up pet waste daily (litter trays must be changed daily, and dog waste must be picked up immediately and placed in the designated bins), and disinfect their living space weekly (using the quaternary ammonium cleaners recommended by ANU). Accommodation Services conducts random inspections each quarter; in 2023 it inspected pet-owning units 214 times and found 29 breaches (13.5%), the most common being litter trays not cleaned promptly (12 cases) and rubbish not sorted (8 cases).

On **community relations**, ANU encourages residents to proactively inform neighbours using a "pet-friendly sign". Residents can display a yellow circular sign on their door (provided free of charge by Accommodation Services) indicating "pet in this unit". If a neighbour raises reasonable concerns (such as allergies or fear), Accommodation Services arranges a mediation meeting and may require the resident to adjust the pet's activity times (for example, restricting outings to 10:00–16:00). An anonymous 2022 survey of pet-owning residents found that 83% of respondents felt the pet improved their mental health, but 12% of neighbours said they were disturbed by noise or odours.

### Area Restrictions for Taking Pets Outside

ANU has designated a pet-only exercise area on campus: the green space along "Sullivan's Creek" on the southern side of the campus (about 1.2 hectares). Residents may not bring pets into the following areas: teaching buildings (including classrooms, laboratories and libraries), dining halls, sports venues (including gyms and swimming pools) and anywhere within 5 metres of children's playground equipment on campus. Pets taken into a restricted area in breach of the rules are recorded on the first occasion; from the second occasion onwards, each incident carries a A$100 fine. In 2023, 31 such incidents were recorded, with fines totalling A$1,800.

## Seasonal Policy Adjustments and Special Periods

ANU's pet policy is temporarily tightened during certain periods. During **exam weeks** (three weeks each in June and November every year), no pet (including ESAs) may leave its living unit between 22:00 and 07:00, to reduce noise disturbance. During **campus event days** (such as O-Week and graduation ceremonies), the pet-free zone is extended to the entire central campus square (around University Avenue), and fines for violations are doubled.

During **natural disasters** (such as the 2020 bushfires in the ACT), ANU activates an emergency accommodation plan, and pet-owning residents must register a pet evacuation plan in advance. During the January 2020 bushfires, ANU coordinated 20 pet-friendly temporary accommodation sites, housing 15 cats and 8 dogs. Accommodation Services recommends that residents take out pet insurance (about A$150–400 per year) to cover sudden illness or evacuation costs.

## Common Violation Cases and How They Are Handled

ANU Accommodation Services publishes a Pet Management Annual Report each year listing typical violation cases. **Case 1** (2022): a resident secretly kept a hamster in a shared apartment at Bruce Hall and was reported by a roommate. Under the policy, the resident was fined A$200 (A$50 per day × 4 days) and had to remove the hamster from campus within 48 hours. **Case 2** (2023): an ESA holder failed to clean up their dog's waste promptly, causing an odour in the public corridor. After two warnings went unheeded, their ESA permission was suspended for 30 days and the animal was required to be temporarily boarded off campus.

**Consequences of repeat violations**: three violations accumulated within 90 days result in a "final warning" and the risk of termination of the accommodation contract. In 2023, five accommodation contracts were terminated for pet-related violations — three involving unregistered pets and two involving excessive ESA noise. Residents whose contracts are terminated must move out within 14 days and may be placed on ANU's accommodation blacklist (valid for 12 months).

## FAQ

### Q1: Can I keep a cat in ANU accommodation?
Yes, but only in a standalone studio or a townhouse with a courtyard, and you must submit a complete application (including vaccination records and a behavioural assessment report). 2023 data shows that about 68% of cat applications were approved, with insufficient space the main reason for rejection. Cats must be kept indoors and are not allowed to roam freely in public areas.

### Q2: What is the difference between an emotional support animal (ESA) and an ordinary pet in ANU accommodation?
An ESA may live in a traditional residential hall (such as a single room), whereas an ordinary pet can only live in a standalone studio; ESA owners do not pay the A$15-per-week pet deposit, but both are subject to the same noise management standards. An ESA requires a recommendation letter from a licensed mental health professional, and it has no automatic right to enter teaching buildings or examination rooms.

### Q3: What procedures do international students need to follow to bring a pet from China to ANU?
You must meet the import quarantine requirements of the Australian Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry (DAFF): dogs must complete a rabies vaccination 30 days before entering the country and hold an import permit (2023 standard). Pet records must be translated into English by a NAATI-accredited translation service. In addition, the student visa conditions do not recognise an ESA as an assistance animal, so it cannot be used to apply for a visa waiver.
