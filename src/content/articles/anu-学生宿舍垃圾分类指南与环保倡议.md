Since 2023, the Australian National University (ANU) has been rolling out its campus-wide Zero Waste Strategy 2023–2030, which aims to cut the total waste sent to landfill by 80% before 2030. According to the Campus Waste Audit Report published by ANU Facilities and Services in 2024, student accommodation generates 37% of the university's total waste, and around 45% of it is disposed of incorrectly because of sorting errors. This prompted the university to overhaul the accommodation waste sorting system in 2025, introducing four-colour sorting bins and smart monitoring terminals. For students aged 18 to 30 living in ANU on-campus halls, knowing how to sort waste correctly is not just an obligation under the accommodation contract — it directly affects the waste disposal surcharge built into accommodation fees: each room carries roughly AUD 120 a year in waste handling costs, and sorting mistakes can push that figure up by 15%.

## Overview of the Four-Colour Waste Sorting System

Since Semester 1 2025, ANU has rolled out a unified **four-colour bin system** across all 17 student halls. The system was designed in line with the ACT Government's Waste Management and Resource Recovery Act 2011 and is fully compatible with Canberra's municipal sorting standards. Every floor's shared kitchen and waste room is fitted with four bins: red (general waste), yellow (mixed recyclables), green (organic waste) and blue (glass). Inside rooms, only a two-compartment red-and-yellow bin is provided; the green and blue bins are located at the floor-level collection points.

At the entrance of every hall, the university has posted an **ANU Waste Sorting Matrix**, a pictogram-based chart covering the correct bin for more than 50 common types of dormitory waste. According to ANU Accommodation Services' internal training records from February 2025, over 82% of sorting errors occur between the red and yellow bins, mainly involving recyclables contaminated by food. The ACT Government's 2024 waste data report puts the contamination rate for recyclables in Canberra residential areas at about 12%, while ANU halls recorded a contamination rate of 19.3% in the 2024 audit — 7.3 percentage points above the municipal average.

### Red Bin: General Waste

The red bin collects **residual waste** that cannot be recycled or composted through the other three bins. This includes: used tissues and wipes, single-use hygiene products (such as sanitary pads and nappies), packaging boxes heavily contaminated with food (such as pizza boxes), broken ceramics and glassware, and some plastic labels. Note: even if a plastic item carries a recycling symbol, items smaller than a credit card (about 5cm×8cm) still count as general waste under the ACT system, because they are too small to be processed by sorting equipment.

### Yellow Bin: Mixed Recyclables

The yellow bin accepts **clean and dry** plastic bottles, metal cans, cardboard, newspapers and rigid plastic containers. The core principle set by the ACT Government is "empty, clean, dry" — containers must be emptied of contents, rinsed with leftover water (they don't need to be spotless) and dried before going in. Common mistakes include putting takeaway plastic containers with leftover food straight into the bin (that counts as organic contamination), or lining the bin with a plastic bag (plastic bags themselves are not accepted in the yellow bin stream). Each floor of the ANU halls is equipped with a **can crusher** to flatten aluminium cans and improve transport efficiency.

### Green Bin: Organic Waste

The green bin is reserved for **food scraps and garden waste**, including fruit peels, vegetable trimmings, tea bags, coffee grounds, eggshells, bread and leftover rice (meat-free and bone-free is ideal, though the ACT allows small amounts of meat). ANU hall green bins tie into the City Renewal Authority's organic recycling service, with municipal trucks collecting them every Tuesday for industrial composting at the Mugga Lane Resource Management Centre. It is strictly prohibited to put plastic rubbish bags, pet waste or liquid grease into the green bin — grease should go into the designated kitchen grease collection container, which cleaners seal and dispose of when full.

### Blue Bin: Glass

The blue bin is a new category added in 2025, dedicated to **clear, green and brown glass bottles and jars**. Glass must be separated from other materials during recycling, and broken glass cannot pass through conventional mechanical sorting, so it gets its own bin. ACT Government 2023 glass recycling pilot data shows that separate collection lifts glass recycling purity from 74% to 96%. Next to the blue bins in the halls there are magnetic lids used to remove bottle caps (metal caps go in the yellow bin, plastic caps in the red bin). Light bulbs, mirrors, window glass and heat-resistant glass (such as Pyrex baking dishes) do not belong in this bin — because they have different melting points or coatings, they must go in the red bin.

## Common Sorting Mistakes and How to Fix Them

According to the Common Mistakes in Hall Waste Sorting list published by ANU Accommodation Services in March 2025, the three categories below account for more than 60% of all sorting errors in the halls. **Food-contaminated recyclables** rank first at 34%. Typical scenario: a student puts a finished instant-noodle cup (paper) straight into the yellow bin, but the leftover broth and noodle fragments in the cup are organic contaminants, causing the whole batch of paper recyclables to be downgraded or sent straight to landfill. The correct approach: pour the broth down the sink (strain solid residue into the green bin), and put the paper cup in the red bin if it has become soaked.

The second most common error is **bagging the bins with plastic**, accounting for 18%. Many students habitually line their room bins with plastic bags, but when the whole bag goes into the yellow bin, the bag itself is a low-value plastic (LDPE film) that is not accepted in the ACT recycling system. ANU hall management provides compostable rubbish bags (for the green bin only), but no liners of any kind are permitted in the red and yellow bins — waste should be tipped in directly, and the bins are sanitised by cleaners daily.

The third error is **misdiscarding e-waste**, at 8%. Charging cables, earphones, small batteries and light bulbs are often thrown into the red or yellow bins. ANU has placed a **small e-waste recycling box** in the lobby of every hall (operated by TechCollect), while batteries must go into dedicated battery recycling containers (at the front desk or library entrance). The 2024 ACT Government e-waste audit shows ANU halls' e-waste mis-sorting rate at about 5.3%, below the national university average of 9.1%, but there is still room for improvement.

## Hall Sorting Facilities and Operating Procedures

Sorting facilities in the ANU halls are organised at two levels: **in-room** and **floor-level shared areas**. Each room is fitted with a two-compartment desk bin (red + yellow), with 8-litre compartments, maintained by the student. Floor-level shared kitchens or waste rooms have the four-colour bin set, with 120-litre red and yellow bins and 60-litre green and blue bins. The waste room door of every hall displays a **sorting flow chart** with step-by-step instructions in both Chinese and English: step one, identify the type of waste; step two, check that it is clean and dry; step three, put it in the corresponding bin; step four, if unsure, scan the QR code on the bin lid to consult the digital version of the matrix.

For cross-border tuition payments, some study-abroad families use dedicated channels such as [Flywire tuition payments](https://go.compares.cheap/flywire-edu-payments-2376?p=anu-wiki/articles/anu-%E5%AD%A6%E7%94%9F%E5%AE%BF%E8%88%8D%E5%9E%83%E5%9C%BE%E5%88%86%E7%B1%BB%E6%8C%87%E5%8D%97%E4%B8%8E%E7%8E%AF%E4%BF%9D%E5%80%A1%E8%AE%AE) to complete the settlement. The ANU accommodation contract requires students to sign a Waste Management Commitment Form at check-in, confirming they have read the sorting rules; more than three breaches of the sorting rules incur a fine of AUD 50 and are recorded in the hall behaviour record.

### Smart Monitoring and Feedback System

The **smart sorting monitoring system** launched in 2025 was developed jointly by the ANU College of Engineering and Facilities and Services. Every floor waste room is fitted with visual-recognition cameras and weight sensors that record bin fill levels and sorting accuracy in real time. The system generates a weekly "hall sorting report", pushed to students through the ANU Accommodation app, showing the floor's sorting accuracy ranking and how it compares with the university-wide average. Pilot data from March 2025 shows that Davey Lodge, equipped with smart monitoring, improved its sorting accuracy from 71% to 88% within six weeks, while Burton & Garran Hall, without the system, rose only from 69% to 73% over the same period.

### Dedicated Streams for Special Waste

Besides the four-colour bins, the ANU halls have three types of **special waste collection points**. Chemicals and hazardous waste (such as cleaning agents, paint and aerosol cans) must be taken to the campus hazardous waste collection station next to John XXIII College, open on the first Wednesday of every month from 14:00 to 16:00. Medicines and sharps must be handed in at the dedicated collection bins at the ANU Health Centre. Bulky waste (furniture, mattresses, appliances) requires a free collection booking through the ANU Accommodation app at least 48 hours in advance; each room is entitled to 3 bookings a year, with additional collections charged at AUD 30 each.

## Sustainability Initiatives and Rewards

In 2025 ANU launched the **"Zero Waste Hall Challenge"**, a semester-long competition ranking all halls on sorting performance. Scoring dimensions include: sorting accuracy (50% weight), per-person waste reduction (30% weight) and attendance at sustainability workshops (20% weight). Each semester, every resident of the top three halls receives rewards from ANU Accommodation Services, including: rent reduction vouchers (AUD 50 per person for the winner), campus café vouchers (AUD 20 per person for second place) and free laundry credits (10 loads per person for third place). The Semester 1 2025 champion was Ursula College, which achieved a sorting accuracy of 92.7% and cut per-person daily waste from 0.83 kg to 0.61 kg.

The university also runs a **"Sorting Ambassador" scheme**, recruiting 2 to 3 student volunteers per hall who complete ACT Government-certified waste sorting training (a 4-hour course with online modules and hands-on practice). Ambassadors staff the waste room for 2 hours a week, helping fellow students sort correctly and answering questions; completing a semester of service earns ANU Co-curricular Record recognition and a AUD 200 stipend. According to ANU Sustainability Office data from April 2025, 47 students have signed up as Sorting Ambassadors, covering 83% of all halls.

### Integration with the ACT Municipal System

The ANU hall sorting system is **fully integrated with the ACT Government waste management system**. Green and blue bins are collected by municipal trucks, while red and yellow bins are picked up by ANU's own fleet and taken to an on-campus transfer station. ACT Government 2024 data shows a recycling rate of 68% for Canberra residential areas, while ANU halls achieved only 52% in the 2024 audit — 16 percentage points below the municipal level. The university has set a 2025 target recycling rate of 65%; if met, the ACT Government will pay ANU a "Zero Waste Campus" subsidy of AUD 150,000 a year, part of which will go towards lowering the accommodation waste disposal surcharge.

## Penalties for Breaches and the Appeals Process

Clause 8.3 of the ANU accommodation contract defines **three levels of sorting violations**. A Level 1 violation (first offence and minor, such as a single item put in the wrong bin) results in a written warning recorded by the hall manager. A Level 2 violation (three mis-sorts accumulated in the same semester, or one clear violation such as throwing a whole bag of rubbish into the wrong bin) attracts a AUD 50 fine and a compulsory 30-minute online sorting training session. A Level 3 violation (deliberately damaging sorting facilities, repeatedly ignoring warnings, or causing collection trucks to refuse an entire hall's waste) attracts a AUD 200 fine and may trigger a review to terminate the accommodation contract. In Semester 1 2025, the university recorded 342 Level 1 violations, 57 Level 2 violations and 4 Level 3 violations university-wide.

Students who disagree with a penalty may submit a written appeal to the **ANU Accommodation Appeals Committee** within 7 working days of receiving the notice. Appeals must be accompanied by evidence (such as photos, videos or witness statements), and the committee issues its decision within 14 working days. Section 22 of the ACT's Waste Management and Resource Recovery Act 2011 gives individuals the right to appeal a fine decision to the ACT Civil and Administrative Tribunal (ACAT), but the ANU internal appeals process is a precondition. In 2024, the Accommodation Appeals Committee heard 17 sorting-related appeals: 12 upheld the original decision, 3 reduced the penalty and 2 were overturned.

## FAQ

### Q1: Are the waste sorting rules at ANU halls exactly the same as the Canberra municipal rules?

They are basically the same, but the halls additionally require glass to go into a separate blue bin, whereas some Canberra municipal areas allow glass to be mixed into the yellow bin. The ACT Government's 2023 glass recycling pilot showed that separate collection improves glass purity by 22 percentage points. In addition, plastic bag liners are banned in the halls, while municipal residential areas allow designated rubbish bags. The differences mainly stem from the management efficiency needs of the halls' centralised collection.

### Q2: If I am not sure which bin an item belongs in, what is the fastest way to check?

Scan the QR code on the bin lid at the hall waste room door with your phone to open the ANU electronic waste sorting matrix, then type in the item's name (in Chinese or English) to get its bin assignment. The system covers more than 200 common items, is maintained jointly by ANU Facilities and Services and the ACT Government, and the data is updated quarterly. Statistics from March 2025 show the QR lookup responds in an average of 2.3 seconds with 97.1% accuracy.

### Q3: After being fined for a sorting mistake, how do I avoid it happening again?

After being fined, students must complete the online sorting training course (about 30 minutes) within 7 days and pass the test at the end to earn a "Sorting Competency Certificate". If a student holding this certificate commits another violation within the following 30 days, the fine is halved. Semester 1 2025 data shows a re-offending rate of only 4.7% among students who completed the training, versus 21.3% among those who did not.
