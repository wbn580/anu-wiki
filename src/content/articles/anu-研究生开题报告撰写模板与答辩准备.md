In the graduate degree requirements of the Australian National University (ANU), the Research Proposal is the key document that determines whether research can proceed smoothly. According to the Graduate Research Projects Handbook released by the ANU Policy and Projects Office in 2024, all Doctor of Philosophy (PhD) and Master of Research (MRes) candidates must submit a formal research proposal signed by their supervisor within 12 months of enrolment (full-time); those who do not pass face the risk of extended or terminated enrolment. Meanwhile, QS World University Rankings 2025 data shows that ANU ranked 30th globally for research citations and 25th for academic reputation, and its research proposal review standards are widely regarded as a model for the Commonwealth research university system. Drawing on the official ANU template, the defence process and recent review data, this article provides a structured guide to writing and preparation.

## Structural Requirements of the Research Proposal

The official ANU research proposal template is standardised by the Graduate Research Centre (GRC) and covers six core sections. Under the Research Proposal Template v3.2 released by GRC in 2024, each section has a clear word limit and content requirements.

**Title page**: must include the research title, candidate's name, student ID, department and the supervisor's signature. The title should be no more than 20 English words and must match the final thesis title. During departmental review, formatting errors on the title page account for 12% of returned research proposals (GRC 2024 internal statistics).

**Abstract**: 200-300 words, covering the research background, core questions, methodology and expected contribution. GRC recommends listing 2-3 keywords in the abstract so the review committee can quickly locate the research field. The clarity of the abstract directly affects the pass rate of the committee's initial screening; according to 2023 data from the ANU College of Arts and Social Sciences, 34% of applications with substandard abstracts were returned for revision at the first round.

**Research questions and hypotheses**: this section must present 1-3 specific, testable questions, together with a theoretical framework or hypotheses. The ANU review committee places particular emphasis on logical consistency between the questions and hypotheses, avoiding mismatches such as "broad questions with narrow hypotheses". The number and quality of research questions is the most heavily weighted section of the review score, accounting for 35% of the total (GRC 2024 review criteria).

## Literature Review and Theoretical Framework

The literature review is the most time-consuming part of the research proposal, requiring candidates to demonstrate command of the frontiers of their field. The ANU Library's 2024 guide states that the review should cover core journal articles published in the last 5 years and cite at least 30-50 references.

**Principles for writing the literature review** follow a "funnel" structure: start from the broad context and progressively narrow to the research gap. The review committee looks for candidates who can identify sub-fields that have not been adequately explored, rather than simply listing existing results. For example, in environmental science, ANU Fenner School review records from 2023 show that successful proposals cited an average of 42 references, 80% of them from publications in the last 3 years.

**Theoretical framework**: candidates must identify the core theory or model they will use and explain its suitability. The ANU College of Arts and Social Sciences requires candidates to cite at least 1-2 classic theories in the framework section and explain why those theories fit the current research better than alternatives. A missing or weak theoretical framework is the second most common reason proposals are sent back for major revision, accounting for 28% of cases (GRC 2024 revision data).

In practice, candidates can organise citations using the ANU Library's reference management tools (such as EndNote or Zotero) and run systematic searches in databases such as Scopus or Web of Science. Some candidates also use tools like [Trip.com student flights](https://invl.me/clngebt) to arrange fieldwork travel when accessing cross-border academic resources, supplementing the local data that the literature cannot cover.

## Research Methods and Data Sources

The research methods section must explain in detail the process of data collection, analysis and validation. ANU colleges differ in their methodological requirements, but all require candidates to demonstrate the feasibility and ethical compliance of their methods.

**Quantitative methods**: specify the sample size, sampling strategy and statistical tests. For example, the ANU College of Business and Economics 2024 template requires candidates to justify their sample size calculation (for example using G*Power software) and explain how missing data will be handled. The review committee takes a strict line on proposals with insufficient sample sizes or inappropriate statistical methods; in 2023, 19% of College of Business and Economics proposals were sent back for additional analysis on these grounds.

**Qualitative methods**: specify the interview or observation protocol design, coding strategy and validity and reliability checks. The ANU College of Arts and Social Sciences requires candidates to attach a draft interview guide or observation schedule in the methods section and to explain how the reproducibility of the data will be ensured. Ethics approval for qualitative research (the Human Ethics Application) must be completed before the proposal is submitted; otherwise the proposal is returned outright.

**Data sources**: distinguish primary from secondary data and note how each will be obtained. The ANU Policy and Projects Office requires that when using government open data (such as Australian Bureau of Statistics 2023 datasets) or third-party databases (such as World Bank Open Data), the data version number and access date must be cited. The reliability of data sources is a key indicator in the committee's feasibility assessment, accounting for 40% of the methods section score.

## Timeline Planning and Feasibility Analysis

Timeline planning is the core tool for assessing research feasibility in the proposal. ANU requires candidates to submit a month-by-month research plan (Gantt chart) covering the full cycle from confirmation to the final defence.

**The timeline table** must include start and end dates for the literature review, data collection, data analysis, thesis writing and revision stages. The GRC 2024 guide recommends a research cycle of 3-4 years for PhD candidates, with data collection occupying 40%-50% of the total time. Unrealistic timelines (such as allocating only 3 months to data collection) are a common reason proposals are sent back for revision, accounting for 22% of all returned cases in 2023.

**Feasibility analysis** must consider resource access, technical capability, ethics approval and potential risks. For example, candidates using laboratory equipment need to confirm equipment availability in advance and attach a confirmation letter from the laboratory manager. ANU College of Science 2023 data shows that proposals returned for major revision because of inadequate timeline planning and feasibility analysis took an average of 8 weeks to revise, well above the 4-week average for other sections.

**Risk mitigation strategies**: candidates must list at least 3 potential risks (such as delays in data access, technical failures or staff changes) and provide fallback options. The review committee favours candidates who demonstrate "contingency thinking" over proposals that emphasise the value of the research while ignoring execution obstacles.

## Expected Contributions and References

The expected contribution section must clarify the incremental value of the research to the academic field or social practice. The ANU review committee divides contributions into theoretical, methodological and practical contributions, requiring candidates to cover at least two of the three types.

**Theoretical contribution**: explain how the research fills a gap in the existing literature, or corrects or extends existing theory. For example, in economics, ANU Crawford School review records from 2023 show that successful proposals proposed an average of 2.3 theoretical innovations, each supported by specific literature. Vague statements of theoretical contribution are the third most common reason proposals are rejected outright, accounting for 15% of cases.

**Practical contribution**: explain the potential impact of the research findings on policy making, industry practice or social issues. The ANU Policy and Projects Office emphasises in particular that MRes proposals should include at least 1 quantifiable practical contribution indicator (such as the operability of policy recommendations or the expected effect of a technical solution).

**References** must strictly follow APA 7th or Harvard citation formats. GRC sets a minimum of 30 references (PhD) or 20 references (MRes), of which at least 60% should come from peer-reviewed journals. Reference formatting errors accounted for 9% of returned proposals in 2023, but reference management software can substantially reduce this risk.

## Defence Preparation Process

The Proposal Defence is a formal stage of the ANU research degree journey, usually lasting 45-60 minutes, comprising a 15-minute presentation and 30-45 minutes of questions.

**The presentation** requires candidates to present the core content of the proposal with PPT or Prezi, highlighting the research questions, methodology and timeline. The GRC 2024 defence guide recommends 10-12 slides, with information density kept low on each slide. Running over time is a common error, accounting for 18% of negative feedback at defences in 2023.

**The Q&A session** is conducted by the review committee (usually including the supervisor, a departmental representative and 1 external expert). Common question types include: the logic for validating the research hypotheses, the ethical compliance of data access, and the buffer mechanisms in the timeline. ANU College of Arts and Social Sciences 2023 defence records show that candidates were asked an average of 4.7 questions, about 40% of them on methodological details. Candidates can rehearse with a mock defence; the average pass rate for candidates who did a mock defence was 23% higher than for those who did not (GRC 2023 internal data).

**Defence outcomes** fall into three grades: "Pass", "Pass with revisions" and "Fail". GRC 2024 statistics show that around 65% of candidates pass at the first attempt, 30% pass with revisions, and 5% are required to defend again. Candidates who fail must resubmit the proposal and defend again within 3 months.

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Based on the Common Errors in Research Proposals report published by the ANU Graduate Research Office in 2023, the four errors below account for 67% of all returned proposals.

**Overly broad research questions** are the most common error, accounting for 24% of returned proposals. For example, posing "the impact of climate change on Australian agriculture" as a question without specifying crop types, regions or time spans. The review committee recommends narrowing the question to a testable scope, such as "a simulation of the impact of a 2°C temperature increase on wheat yields in Queensland over 2030-2040".

**Methods mismatched with the questions** account for 19% of returned proposals. For example, posing a qualitative research question but using quantitative statistical methods, or vice versa. When writing the methods section, candidates should ensure that every method directly answers at least one research question.

**A non-critical literature review** accounts for 14% of returned proposals. The review committee discourages simple lists of literature and instead expects candidates to demonstrate the ability to evaluate and synthesise existing research — for example, by pointing out the limitations of a classic theory or the room for improvement in a research method.

**Unrealistic timeline planning** accounts for 10% of returned proposals. Common problems include underestimating data collection time, ignoring the ethics approval cycle or leaving no buffer. Candidates should refer to successful proposals from previous years in their department and seek their supervisor's advice.

## FAQ

### Q1: When is the ANU research proposal submission deadline?

PhD candidates must submit their formal research proposal within 12 months of enrolment (full-time); MRes candidates have 9 months. Extension applications require the supervisor's signature and GRC approval, and can extend the deadline to 15 months (PhD) or 12 months (MRes) at most — but only 47% of extension applications were approved in 2023 (GRC 2023 annual report).

### Q2: After a proposal is returned for revision, how long does it usually take to resubmit?

According to GRC 2023 data, the average revision period is 5.7 weeks; proposals returned for timeline problems take an average of 8.2 weeks, while those returned for literature review problems take an average of 4.3 weeks. Candidates should draw up a revision plan with their supervisor immediately after receiving the revision notice.

### Q3: Is there a second chance after failing the defence?

Yes. GRC rules allow candidates who fail the defence to resubmit the proposal and defend again within 3 months. 2023 data shows an 83% pass rate for second attempts, but the pass rate drops to 41% for a third attempt (GRC 2023 internal statistics). Candidates are advised to discuss revision directions individually with each review committee member after a first failure.
