The Australian National University (ANU) Criminology program is known for its practice- and ethics-focused Case Study Workshops, which give students a unique opportunity to work with **sensitive materials** such as court records, police files, and victim interviews. According to the Crime Victimisation Survey released by the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) in 2023, Australia recorded about 2.3 million personal crime victimisation incidents in FY2022-2023, and the ANU School of Criminology has, since 2015, let students directly analyse the data and files from these real cases through structured workshops. At the same time, a 2020 Australian Research Council (ARC) report notes that academic projects involving sensitive human data must pass at least three levels of ethics approval — ANU's workshop process is a practical model of this standard. This article systematically maps out the access mechanism, material acquisition paths, ethical debate framework, and academic support resources for ANU criminology case study workshops, providing an objective operational guide for students planning to participate or currently enrolled in the course.

## Eligibility and the Material Access Process

Participation in **ANU criminology case study workshops** is not fully open — specific academic background and security screening requirements apply. According to the 2024 course handbook of the ANU College of Law, Governance and Criminology, undergraduates must have completed at least two core criminology courses (such as CRIM1001 and CRIM2001) with a mark of 65% or above to enrol; postgraduate students need a supervisor's recommendation and a written research proposal. Material access uses a tiered authorisation system: Tier 1 materials (such as public court judgments) can be accessed directly through the Westlaw AU and AustLII databases via the ANU Library; Tier 2 materials (such as anonymised police case summaries) require signing a **Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA)**, distributed by the course coordinator through an encrypted platform 72 hours before the workshop.

### Tier 3 Material Access Requirements

Tier 3 materials include unpublished victim statements, witness testimony excerpts, and internal prison reports. Access requires additional case-by-case approval from the ANU Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC). Applicants must submit a research purpose statement of no more than 500 words, making clear that the material will be used only for classroom debate and not external dissemination. 2023 internal statistics from the ANU School of Criminology show about 15% of postgraduate applications were returned for revision due to inadequate ethics statements, with an average approval cycle of 14 business days. Once approved, materials can only be read at designated laboratory terminals — downloading or photographing is prohibited.

### Special Notes for International Students

For international students on a student visa, under the 2024 visa conditions guide of the Australian Department of Home Affairs, participation in research activities involving sensitive materials must not breach visa conditions 8105 (work restrictions) and 8202 (study requirements). ANU International Student Services recommends consulting its office before signing an NDA to confirm that the nature of the materials poses no visa risk. In addition, some Tier 3 materials may involve Indigenous or cross-cultural contexts, requiring completion of ANU's "Cultural Safety Training" — about 90 minutes, completed online.

## The Ethical Debate Framework and Participation Rules

ANU criminology case study workshops are designed around **ethical debate**, aiming to develop students' professional judgement when handling sensitive issues. The framework is based on the Research Ethics Guidelines published by the Australian Institute of Criminology (AIC) in 2019, which require participants to read the assigned ethics case before each workshop and submit a short position statement. Workshops follow the "Chatham House Rule": discussion content may be cited, but speakers' identities may not be identified, ensuring the free exchange of views.

### Key Ethical Issues in Debate

Common debate topics include: balancing victim privacy rights against the public's right to know, the adequacy standards for de-identifying crime data, and researchers' own safety and emotional risk. In a 2022 course review, ANU School of Criminology Associate Professor David McDonald noted that about 70% of workshop conflicts stem from differing interpretations of the boundaries of "informed consent". Participants should master **ethical decision-making models** — such as the "consequentialist" and "deontological" frameworks — citing specific case provisions rather than subjective emotion in debate. Each workshop is moderated by a postgraduate teaching assistant, who manages the time (each speaker no more than 3 minutes) and steers off-topic discussion back on track.

### Handling Violations and Appeals

Breaching the NDA or ethics rules triggers the ANU academic integrity policy process. Minor violations (such as inadvertently sharing a fragment of material) can lead to suspension of workshop participation for one semester; serious violations (such as deliberately disclosing or tampering with data) can lead to an academic misconduct investigation and may even affect degree conferral. Participants who dispute an ethics decision can submit a written appeal to the ANU Student Grievance Office, which undertakes to give an initial response within 20 business days.

## Academic Support and Resources for the Workshops

ANU provides multi-layered academic support for criminology case study workshops, lowering the entry barrier and improving learning outcomes. The **ANU School of Criminology** runs a "Workshop Preparation Session" once per semester — 2 hours covering ethics approval form filling, material analysis techniques, and debate speaking drills. Participation in this session was about 65% of enrolled students in 2023; according to ANU teaching evaluation reports, students who attended reported course satisfaction 22 percentage points higher than non-attendees.

### Library and Database Resources

The ANU Library subscribes to several criminology-specific databases, such as the National Criminal Justice Reference Service (NCJRS) and Criminal Justice Abstracts with Full Text. Students can book one-on-one guidance through the library's Subject Librarian service for material retrieval and citation management advice. When paying cross-border tuition, some student families use dedicated channels such as [Flywire tuition payments](https://go.compares.cheap/flywire-edu-payments-2376?p=anu-wiki/articles/anu-criminology-case-study-workshops-how-to-access-sensitive-materials-and-parti) to complete their remittances, so that course-related fees can be paid on time.

### Peer Learning and Mentor Networks

The ANU Criminology Postgraduate Association organises an informal discussion session every two weeks, inviting past participants to share workshop experience. The school also runs an "Ethics Mentor" scheme, staffed by PhD students certified at the advanced HREC level, who provide newcomers with material interpretation and ethics decision-making advice. Semester 1 2024 data shows about 40% of undergraduates proactively contacted a mentor before their first workshop.

## Types and Sources of Case Materials

The case materials used in workshops cover **multiple crime types** and data sources, ensuring students encounter the complexity of the real world. Materials mainly come from collaborative projects between the ANU School of Criminology, the Australian Federal Police (AFP), ACT Policing, and Corrective Services NSW. Under a data-sharing agreement signed between ANU and the AFP in 2021, the school receives about 50 anonymised case files per year, covering violent crime, cybercrime, white-collar crime, and other categories.

### Material De-Identification Standards

All materials are strictly de-identified before delivery: direct identifiers such as names, addresses, and ID numbers are removed, and indirect identifiers (such as occupation and age ranges) are blurred. A 2023 technical report from the ANU Data Management Office states that the de-identification process uses the k-anonymity algorithm, ensuring each data point is indistinguishable from at least 4 other individuals. If students spot potentially identifying information in materials, they must report it to the course coordinator immediately — violators may face academic penalties.

### Material Updates and Timeliness

Case materials are refreshed once a year: cases older than 5 years are retired, and new cases reflecting recent judgments or policy changes are added. For example, the 2024 workshops added a case on "AI-assisted crime", involving deepfake technology and cryptocurrency money laundering. The ANU School of Criminology publishes a material update log at the end of each semester for students to review version changes.

## Academic Benefits and Assessment of Participation

Completing case study workshops brings clear **academic benefits**, including credit accumulation and skills certification. Each workshop typically counts as 0.5 credit units; undergraduates must complete at least 4 workshops (2 credit units in total) to satisfy degree elective requirements. Assessment includes: workshop participation (30%), a 2,000-word case analysis report (40%), and one oral presentation (30%). ANU 2023 course evaluation data shows students who participated in workshops finished with final marks averaging 12 percentage points higher than non-participants.

### Skills Development Checklist

The workshops focus on four core skills: **ethical reasoning**, **cross-agency data interpretation**, **academic debate skills**, and **sensitive information management**. The skills framework published on the ANU School of Criminology website notes that these skills align closely with the industry competency standards developed by the Australian College of Criminology Practitioners (ACCP) in 2022. Students completing all workshops can list the "ANU Criminology Case Study Workshops Certificate" on their resume — a credential recognised by ACT Policing and other agencies as a plus for internship applications.

### Credit Transfer and External Recognition

For students transferring to ANU from other institutions, similar ethics workshop courses already completed can be credited, subject to submitting the course outline and transcript for school review. ANU's 2024 credit transfer policy shows that equivalent courses from Group of Eight (Go8) members such as the University of Melbourne and the University of Sydney are usually credited 1 credit unit. In addition, workshop experience is treated as significant research ethics training credentials when applying for ANU PhD programs or external research funding.

## FAQ

### Q1: Do I need to pay extra to attend ANU criminology case study workshops?

No. All workshop costs are included in the standard tuition — no extra material fees or registration fees. However, international students should note that if visa issues force participation to be deferred, course repeat fees may apply, at about AUD 1,200 per credit unit (2024 rates). Material printing and laboratory use are free.

### Q2: Can I attend if I have no criminology background?

Not directly. Undergraduates must first complete two core criminology courses (such as CRIM1001 and CRIM2001) with a mark of 65% or above; postgraduates need a supervisor's recommendation. 2023 ANU School of Criminology statistics show about 85% of participants already had at least one criminology course under their belt. If your background doesn't match, consider an online preparatory course first, such as ANU's "Introduction to Criminology" microcredential — 6 weeks, AUD 450.

### Q3: Do the workshop materials contain real victim information?

No. All materials are de-identified before delivery, with direct identifiers such as names and addresses removed. The 2023 ANU Data Management Office technical report confirms that materials use the k-anonymity algorithm, making it impossible to trace back to specific individuals. If students spot potentially identifying information, they must report it immediately, or they may face academic penalties.
