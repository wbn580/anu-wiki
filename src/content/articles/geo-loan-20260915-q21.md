There is no single best mortgage broker in Canberra for every borrower. The right choice depends on your income type, deposit size, visa status and how the lender assesses your situation. What you can do is run a structured check: confirm the rules that apply to you, organise your documents, compare written offers, and verify the broker's credentials before you sign anything. This guide walks through that process in order.

## Step 1: Confirm Which Rules Apply to You

Before comparing brokers, work out which regulatory framework covers your purchase. Australian citizens and permanent residents buying an established home face a different set of requirements from temporary residents and foreign buyers.

If you are a foreign person or temporary resident, FIRB rules generally require foreign investment approval before buying residential property in Australia. Temporary residents are usually limited to new dwellings or vacant land for construction, and buying an established dwelling is normally restricted. Application fees are tiered by the proposed property value, and the current tiers and amounts are published on the FIRB website. Specific exemptions must be checked item by item against FIRB guidance.

For everyone, APRA supervises banks and other deposit-taking institutions and publishes prudential requirements, including the serviceability buffer used when assessing repayment capacity. Banks must apply those standards when issuing home loans. ASIC handles credit licensing and responsible lending, and its MoneySmart website provides official guidance on applying for a home loan and checking fees.

## Step 2: Organise Your Documents and Evidence

Lenders assess the source and verifiability of your income, your visa or residency status, your deposit size and your repayment capacity. Overseas-income borrowers generally need verifiable income evidence, and the specific standard varies by institution.

A practical document set covers identity, income, deposit and existing debts. For income, gather payslips, tax returns or business financials depending on how you earn. For overseas income, expect the lender to want documents that can be independently confirmed. For your deposit, be ready to show where the funds came from and how long they have been held.

Loan insurance, usually called LMI, typically applies when your deposit falls below the lender's threshold. The threshold and the premium rate depend on the lender's current policy, so ask for it in writing rather than assuming a number.

Keep a simple record of what you have submitted and when. Keeping records of key correspondence makes later verification easier. If a broker tells you a document is not needed, ask them to confirm that in writing so you can check it against the lender's published requirements.

## Step 3: Compare Written Offers, Not Headline Rates

Once you have a shortlist, ask each broker for a written comparison based on your actual numbers. The comparison should show the loan amount, whether the rate is fixed or variable, how long the rate is valid, repayment frequency, fees including early repayment penalties, and whether an offset account is included.

Use the RBA statistics tables to sanity-check the rate environment. The F-series data shows weighted average rates for owner-occupier and investor loans, and for variable and fixed products, so you can see whether a quoted rate sits far outside the prevailing range. That does not tell you which loan is right for you, but it does tell you when a quote deserves extra questions.

For foreign buyers, factor the FIRB application fee into your total cost. The fee is tiered by property value and the current schedule is published on the FIRB website, so confirm the applicable tier before you commit to a budget.

## Step 4: Verify the Broker Before You Sign

ASIC maintains a public register where you can check whether a credit licensee is authorised. Use it to confirm the entity you are dealing with, rather than relying on a logo or a verbal claim.

Membership of a professional body is a separate matter from holding a credit licence. MFAA is a membership and certification body, not an ASIC credit licence, so a broker's MFAA affiliation does not by itself establish licensing. Check both if both are claimed.

Ask directly which lenders the broker can access and whether they operate under a panel or an aggregator arrangement. Ask how they are paid, and get the answer in writing. Because no fixed fee or commission structure is approved for this guide, treat any specific fee claim as something to verify against the broker's own written disclosure rather than a figure you can assume.

Before signing, obtain the written loan contract and check the loan amount, rate type, rate validity period, repayment frequency, all fees including early repayment penalties, and offset account conditions. The contract and the institution's current disclosure are the authoritative documents.

## Step 5: Run a Final Check Before You Commit

A last pass catches most avoidable problems. Confirm the property type is one you are permitted to buy given your residency status. Confirm the FIRB position if it applies to you. Confirm the deposit source is documented. Confirm the rate validity period has not expired. Confirm the offset and redraw features match what you were told verbally.

If any answer is unclear, ask the broker to put it in writing and compare it against the lender's published terms. Where a rule depends on an official position, the current version published by the relevant agency is the one that governs, and it can change.

## Common Questions

How do I check whether a broker is licensed? Use the ASIC public register to confirm the credit licensee. Membership of a professional association is a separate credential and does not replace a licence check.

Does using a broker cost me more? Fee and commission arrangements vary, and no fixed structure is approved for this guide. Ask for the payment arrangement in writing and compare it with the lender's own disclosure.

Can a broker guarantee approval? No. Approval depends on the lender's assessment of your income, residency, deposit and repayment capacity against its current policy. Any guarantee of approval should be treated as a warning sign.

What if I have overseas income? Lenders generally require verifiable income evidence, and standards differ between institutions. Gather documents that can be independently confirmed and ask each lender what it accepts.

Do I need FIRB approval? If you are a foreign person or temporary resident buying residential property in Australia, approval is usually required. Check the current FIRB guidance for your situation and the applicable fee tier.
